Bitcoin headed 'in bullish direction' as investors bet on Fed pause: Chart of the Day

03 Oct 2026

Bitcoin spent the first half of 2026 getting beaten up, and by Friday it was hovering near $85,000, having clawed back most of what it lost. That sentence contains the whole tension of the moment, because the thing that was supposed to hurt crypto — rising Treasury yields, the government's own borrowing costs creeping upward — didn't kill it. Bitcoin just closed its strongest quarter since 2024, up 43%, while the yield on short-term government debt kept climbing. That shouldn't happen if the old story about bitcoin were true.

The old story went like this: bitcoin is a risk asset, and risk assets fall when safe assets start paying real money. A Treasury bond is a loan you make to the US government. The "yield" is the interest it pays you back. When that number goes up, parking cash in government debt gets more attractive, and speculative stuff — crypto, tech stocks, a friend's kombucha franchise — gets less attractive by comparison. For most of bitcoin's life, that relationship held. Yields up, bitcoin down. Simple.

Except the relationship has been quietly breaking. Sean Farrell, who runs digital asset strategy at Fundstrat, pointed at a more specific number this week: the 2-year real yield, which is the interest on a two-year Treasury bond after subtracting inflation. Strip inflation out and you're measuring what you actually get for lending the government money — your true reward for playing it safe. That number has been falling. And if it keeps falling, the case for holding short-term government debt weakens, which is a polite way of saying the competition bitcoin faces for investor dollars gets less intimidating. Not because bitcoin got better. Because the alternative got worse.

Here's where the story gets interesting, and where most people will get it wrong. The obvious read on Friday's move was that the Fed blinked. A weak jobs report landed — fewer Americans working than expected — and more than three-quarters of market participants now expect the central bank to hold rates steady at its October meeting rather than raise them. Lower rates mean cheaper money, cheaper money means speculation, speculation means bitcoin. That's the version that fits on a bumper sticker.

But Farrell isn't making that argument. He's making a stranger one. Continued stress in sovereign bonds and credit markets — the plumbing underneath the entire global financial system — could still trigger a short-term drawdown in crypto. In other words, the thing that's supposedly helping bitcoin right now could also, at any moment, take it out back. His framing of that risk is the most revealing sentence in the whole story: "The 'glass half full' perspective is that the more stress that gets priced in without breaking BTC, the better the forward risk/reward becomes." Translation — every time the system creaks and bitcoin doesn't crack, the market learns something. It learns that bitcoin might be more durable than the models say. That's not a price forecast. That's a slow accumulation of evidence.

Which is why the Citi revision matters more than it looks. Analyst Alex Saunders raised his base-case bitcoin target to $113,000 from $82,000. A base case is what an analyst thinks happens if nothing unusual occurs — it's the boring middle of the distribution, not the dream scenario. When the boring middle moves up 38% in one revision, the analyst isn't predicting a rally. He's admitting the old middle was wrong.

Now the part nobody wants to say out loud: seasonality. Farrell notes October has historically been crypto's strongest month, with the highest median and average returns and roughly an 80% win rate. An 80% win rate over a handful of Octobers is not a law of nature. It's a pattern with a sample size small enough to fit in a spreadsheet cell. But patterns move markets precisely because enough people believe them, and right now enough people believe them. Seasonality is a self-fulfilling story with a calendar attached.

So what actually changed this week? Not bitcoin's code. Not its adoption. Not a single line of the protocol. What changed is that two forces that used to push in the same direction — government bond yields and crypto prices — are now pushing in opposite ones, and the market is being forced to pick which story it believes. Either bitcoin is still a risk asset that will get dragged down the moment credit markets seize, or it's becoming something else, something that holds its ground while the bond market wobbles. Those two possibilities cannot both be true forever.

The honest answer is that nobody knows yet, and the people closest to the trade are hedging in plain sight. Farrell's own note contains both the bullish case and the drawdown warning in the same paragraph. That's not indecision. That's what it looks like when a thesis is mid-transition — when the old model has stopped working but the new one hasn't been proven. Bitcoin at $85,000, up 43% in a quarter, with yields rising and a Fed that might hold steady, is a market that has stopped asking whether crypto is risky and started asking what, exactly, it's risky relative to. That question doesn't have a chart answer. It has a decade-long answer, and the decade isn't over.

Bitget Hack Update: XRP, Bitcoin, and North Korean Link

03 Oct 2026

The first three hours are the tell. Twenty-three outbound transfers, moving $387 million across four different blockchains, and not one of them lands on an exchange where a compliance officer might freeze it. Ethereum takes 49.7 percent. The XRP Ledger takes 40.8 percent. Zcash, the privacy coin, absorbs 7.6 percent, Tron 1.8 percent. To anyone watching the raw ledger, this looks like chaos — money spraying in every direction. It isn't. It's a withdrawal, executed in the open, by people who understood exactly what the surveillance could and couldn't see.

Here's the mechanism, because the mechanism is the whole story. When you steal tokens, you steal a liability. A stolen XRP sitting in a wallet is radioactive: the moment it touches a regulated exchange, that exchange's compliance team — the people whose job is to match incoming deposits against a list of known theft addresses — flags it, freezes it, and the money is gone. So the attackers don't sell. A sale requires a counterparty with a bank account and a name. Instead they deposit the XRP into a cross-chain liquidity protocol, which is a pool of funds that will swap you one asset on one blockchain for a different asset on another, no order book, no exchange, no identity check. What comes out the other side is Bitcoin, on a different network, in a wallet that has no visible connection to the XRP that went in. The tokens never touched a place that could ask who they belonged to. That's the trick. Not hacking an exchange twice — hacking one exchange and then laundering the proceeds through plumbing that was built to be trustless and is therefore also built to be blameless.

Chainalysis matched the deposits to their payouts — tens of millions of dollars over roughly thirty-six hours — and the trail now ends at attacker-controlled Bitcoin addresses the firm monitors. Which is a strange kind of victory. They know where the money is. They just can't take it back.

This is the third time this year the same crew has done this. On April 1, hackers drained $285 million from Drift Protocol, and Drift said the operation involved months of social engineering, including in-person meetings — actual humans, sitting across actual tables, building trust before pulling the plug. On April 18, a bridge exploit at KelpDAO took $292 million, and both TRM and LayerZero tied it to TraderTraitor, a unit affiliated with Lazarus, the North Korean state hacking operation that has been funding the regime through crypto theft for years. Those two hacks alone were 76 percent of all crypto hack losses through April. Add Bitget's $387 million and the 2026 haul crosses $1 billion. In 2025, the same actors stole over $2 billion. This is not a crime wave. It's a revenue stream, and it's growing.

Bitget's CEO, Gracy Chen, didn't wait for the forensics. Shortly after the September 24 breach she pointed at North Korea, citing IP addresses that matched a DPRK group's known VPN choices. That's a small detail with a large implication: the attackers' operational security has a fingerprint. They reuse infrastructure. Which means the defenders aren't hunting ghosts — they're hunting an organization with habits, and habits can be anticipated.

Which brings us to the part of this story that isn't about North Korea at all. Chainalysis said it built custom automations using its in-house AI to trace the cross-chain swaps, and that what would have taken more than twenty hours of manual bridge reconciliation — a human sitting at a screen, matching deposits on one chain to withdrawals on another, block by block — shrank to under ten minutes. Investigators still set the logic and reviewed the results. Read that sentence twice, because it's the honest version of how AI enters this story. The machine isn't deciding who the criminal is. It's doing the tedium: reconciling thousands of transactions across four ledgers that don't share a clock or a format, at a speed no analyst could match. The analyst still has to know what to look for.

And that's the asymmetry that actually matters. The attackers used automation too — Chainalysis's own statement says North Korea is "increasingly using sophisticated automation to move and obscure stolen funds." Both sides are now running machines against each other, and the defender's machine has a constraint the attacker's doesn't: it has to be right, and it has to be defensible in a courtroom. The attacker's machine just has to be fast. Ten minutes versus twenty hours is a real victory, but it's a victory in a race where the other runner keeps changing shoes.

Zoom out and the picture gets uncomfortable. Crypto's founding promise was that you wouldn't need to trust institutions — the code would enforce the rules, and no bank could freeze you out. That promise is precisely what makes it the ideal treasury for a sanctioned state. Cross-chain bridges exist because blockchains can't talk to each other, and someone has to move value between them; that someone is a liquidity pool with no compliance department, because adding one would defeat the point. Every piece of infrastructure that makes crypto useful to a dissident in a country with frozen banking is the same infrastructure that makes it useful to Lazarus. You cannot sever one without severing the other.

So the industry is left doing forensics after the fact, publishing reports, monitoring addresses it cannot seize, and building faster tracing tools to keep pace with faster laundering. Bitget's $387 million is now the largest crypto hack of 2026 and pushed September losses up 462 percent. The number will be surpassed. The question isn't whether the next bridge gets drained — it's whether the tracing gets fast enough to matter before the money finishes moving. Ten minutes is a start. The attackers only needed thirty-six hours.

Wall Street on the brink of bearish majority after gold’s post-payrolls slide, Main Street abandons bullish bias as metals test support

02 Oct 2026

The strange thing about gold is that it’s supposed to be the asset you reach for when everything else is wobbling. And yet here was a week where the wobbles were everywhere — a war premium baked into oil, a jobs report that missed so badly it looked like a typo — and gold spent most of it sliding. It opened Sunday night at $4,277.90 an ounce. By Friday it had touched $4,110.95, and it closed just fifteen dollars off the session low. The metal that exists to absorb fear refused to absorb any.

To understand why, you have to understand what gold is actually competing against, and it isn’t stocks. Gold pays you nothing. It sits in a vault and glitters. That’s the whole proposition. Which means its price is really a measure of what you’re giving up by holding it — and this week, what you were giving up got more expensive. Long-term Treasury yields, the interest the U.S. government pays to borrow your money for a decade, climbed. When a risk-free bond starts paying real money, the cost of parking capital in a metal that pays zero goes up. Add a firmer dollar — and gold, priced in dollars, gets more expensive for anyone holding yen or euros — and you have two headwinds blowing at once. Normally that’s enough to knock gold down. This week it got a third: higher oil, driven by the U.S.-Iran conflict, which keeps inflation anxiety alive, which keeps the Fed’s finger near the rate-hike button.

That last part is the knot. A rate hike is the Fed raising the price of borrowing money, and it’s the bluntest anti-inflation tool there is. Markets spent the week pricing in the chance of another one in October. So the picture for gold was: a competing asset paying more, a currency making it dearer abroad, and a central bank that might tighten into an oil shock. Sellers took control Monday and mostly never let go.

Then Friday arrived, and the jobs report came in at 29,000 new jobs. For context, the U.S. economy needs something in the neighborhood of 100,000 a month just to keep up with population growth. The unemployment rate ticked up to 4.2%, and July and August were revised lower — meaning the recent past was weaker than we thought. This is the kind of number that should have been rocket fuel for gold. It cut the odds of an October hike. The dollar fell. Yields fell. And gold did rally — briefly. Then it failed to hold above $4,200 and slid hard into the weekend.

Sean Lusk, who runs commercial hedging at Walsh Trading, sounded like a man watching a law of physics break. “I thought, with crude being down and equities being up and the dollar under pressure, we’d be up,” he said. “Initial reaction was positive, but now we’re negative, and you just can’t get any traction.” He called it illogical. He’s right that it’s illogical, and he’s also describing something real: when an asset stops responding to its own good news, that’s information. It tells you the marginal buyer has already bought. Lusk’s read is that gold “just ran its course for a couple of years for now.” Not turning over, exactly. Just unable to sustain a rally. “You want to bottom-feed down here, and it looks okay for a day or two, and then boom: You react bearishly to bullish news.”

The survey data tells the same story from the crowd’s side. Kitco’s weekly poll of thirteen analysts found nearly half of Wall Street turning bearish — only three expected gains, six expected declines. And Main Street, the retail investors who had been bullish since the end of July, finally gave up that bias. That matters more than it sounds. Retail sentiment in gold is a momentum indicator as much as a forecast; when the true believers fold, you’re usually closer to a bottom than a top, but you’re also watching the last source of bid support step away.

Not everyone reads it as the end. Marc Chandler of Bannockburn Global Forex pointed to signals from Fed leadership — Jefferson on the Board, Williams at the New York Fed — suggesting more patience than the market assumed, and noted gold recaptured $4,200 before the weekend. “A move above the $4,280-$4,300 area would boost confidence that a low is in place.” Adam Button at investingLive went neutral, waiting for an October buying opportunity before what he calls the strong seasonals of November through January. Adrian Day, who runs an asset management firm, expects gold a little lower but “not for long,” and framed the real driver as fiscal: an end to the war would let the dollar resume falling “amidst impossible U.S. deficits,” while a Fed pause would give gold relief. Rich Checkan at Asset Strategies International was one of the three bulls, arguing the weak jobs number plus Williams’ comment about no urgency to hike should let gold “soar a little.”

Here’s the part worth sitting with. Every one of those bulls is making a bet about the Fed, the dollar, or the calendar. None of them is making a bet about gold being scarce, or useful, or newly desired. Gold’s case this week wasn’t about gold at all — it was about whether the thing you give up to hold it gets cheaper. And the honest answer from the tape is that right now, with equities melting up and yields elevated, the opportunity cost is still winning. Lusk put it plainly: “All the love is in equities right now, and that’s been it.”

So watch the $4,280 to $4,300 band. If gold clears it, the October seasonal trade has legs and the bulls who stayed patient get paid. If it can’t, then the more interesting question isn’t where gold goes next — it’s what it means that the world’s oldest fear gauge spent a week full of fear and couldn’t find a bid. Either the fear is already priced in, or the market has decided that in a world of 5% yields, the safest thing to own isn’t a metal at all. That’s a bigger story than one week’s range.

Gold’s resilience supports strong outlook, silver faces oversupply, copper could be the big gainer – Deutsche Bank’s Ghali

02 Oct 2026

The most interesting thing about gold right now is not that it went up. It’s that it didn’t go down.

Daniel Ghali, who runs metals research at Deutsche Bank, put it more precisely in a recent interview: the pullback in gold has been “incredibly limited.” Consider what the metal was up against. The US 10-year Treasury yield — the interest rate the American government pays to borrow money for a decade, and the number against which every other investment gets priced — had rocketed past 5%. Crude oil traded above $100 a barrel. The Federal Reserve’s outlook had flipped hard. And yet gold, which pays no interest and therefore should look terrible when safe bonds suddenly pay a lot, hadn’t printed a new low since July. That’s the whole story in one sentence. The asset that is supposed to lose when yields win, refused to lose.

Why does that matter? Because of who was watching. Ghali describes a positioning setup in gold that is the most bearish since October 2021 — meaning the people who trade gold for a living have placed their bets as if the price is going down, and have largely done so already. When everyone who wants to sell has sold, the only direction left is up. That’s the setup. But the setup sits inside a completely different world than the last time it appeared.

Start with the buyers who don’t behave like traders. Central banks — the official sector, in the language of the market — are buying gold at more than double the pace they were in 2021. These aren’t momentum chasers. A reserve manager at a central bank holds a country’s foreign exchange savings and has to decide what to store it in. Gold is the asset that doesn’t belong to any other government. When that manager buys, they tend to hold for years, which removes the metal from the market and quietly tightens the floor under the price. Ghali notes the network of institutional investors who participate in gold has grown roughly 70% since 2021, and the pool of reserves those managers have to allocate has grown too. More buyers, bigger wallets, and a structural reason to buy that didn’t exist before.

Then there’s the bond side, which is where most people get the story wrong. The instinct is to treat gold and Treasuries as competitors: when one pays, the other suffers. Ghali argues the opposite is happening. The bear market in Treasuries — a long stretch of falling bond prices — is itself the thing pushing big institutions to diversify. Think about a pension fund that has spent twenty years piling into alternative assets: private equity, real estate, hedge funds. The problem is that many of those alternatives still carry yield sensitivity, meaning they still move with interest rates, so they don’t actually diversify anything when rates are the thing hurting you. A pension fund that thought it had spread its bets discovers it made the same bet many times. Gold is the asset that doesn’t share that sensitivity. So the bond selloff isn’t competing with gold. It’s feeding it.

Silver is the mirror image, and the contrast is brutal. Earlier this year silver ran to all-time highs. Then came the war in Iran, and a 50% pullback. Ghali’s explanation for the run-up is the part that should make you sit up: physical silver was at its scarcest since the Hunt brothers tried to corner the market almost half a century ago. The Hunts were two Texas oil heirs who, in 1979 and 1980, tried to buy up the world’s silver supply and got crushed when the exchange changed the rules and the price collapsed. That is the comparison. Scarcity that extreme.

That scarcity is gone. Ghali tracks something called free-floating London inventories — the pool of metal sitting in London’s commercial vaults that is actually available to buy, as opposed to metal already spoken for. That pool has climbed back to its highest level since November 2024. The Comex, the New York exchange where metals futures trade, pulled in a tidal wave of imports into the United States and now sits on a stockpile too large for its open interest — too much metal chasing too few contracts. Shanghai inventories have risen too. Three vaults, three cushions, where a year ago there was one thin mattress.

And high prices did what high prices do: they killed demand. Chinese industrial demand for silver in solar panels is probably down by a third in 2026 versus last year, according to Ghali’s read. Solar manufacturers buy silver paste to make the conductive layers of a panel. When silver gets expensive, they thrift it, substitute it, or delay. So the market now has more inventory covering a shrinking deficit — and possibly a physical surplus next year. More metal, less need. That argues for silver underperforming gold, for the ratio between them deteriorating, and for calmer prices. The market keeps waiting for last year’s fireworks. The fuel for them has been hauled away.

Which leaves copper, and here Ghali gets closest to excitement. He calls it the most acutely scarce copper environment on record, dating to the 1980s, and describes a “convex” reaction function — meaning prices won’t rise in a straight line, but will accelerate as the shortage bites, the way a rubber band stiffens just before it snaps. The mechanism is stockpiling. The United States and China have spent years pulling copper into their own warehouses, and together they have locked up roughly 70% of the world’s aboveground inventory. Aboveground is the key word. Copper sits in the ground in Chile and Peru, but getting it out takes permits, water, capital, and a decade. Most of the heavy lifting happens between a processor and its own memory, inside the box. An AI rack is a different animal. Training a large model is not one machine thinking hard; it's thousands of chips passing partial results back and forth, thousands of times a second. If the connections between them are slow, the expensive GPUs sit idle waiting for data. So the fiber requirement per rack doesn't rise by ten percent. It rises by an order of magnitude — a tenfold jump, which in a capital-intensive hardware business is the difference between a commodity and a shortage.

That's the mechanism. Now the two companies standing in front of it.

Sterlite Technologies traces back to the original Sterlite Industries group and became a focused telecom and optical products company after demerging its power transmission business in 2015. The years that followed were brutal. A prolonged global oversupply of optical fiber — too much manufacturing capacity chasing too few orders — dragged the company into repeated quarterly losses as recently as the December 2024 quarter. This is a business where the factory is the product: you build a plant that draws glass into hair-thin strands, and then you wait. That includes ultra-high-density, pre-terminated "AI Whitespace" cabling for connections inside the data center — pre-terminated meaning the connectors are already attached at the factory, so installers aren't splicing thousands of strands by hand inside a hot, crowded hall — and a Celesta IBR cable series carrying up to 6,912 fibers for links between data centers. It was the single largest order in the company's history, and it pushed the open order book to Rs 186.18 billion. The Enterprise and Data Center segment made up about 19% of FY26 revenue; management is targeting 30% for the current fiscal. Sterlite is up roughly 690% for the year through mid-September.

So which is better placed? That's the question the headline asks, and it's the wrong question, or at least a premature one. Here's why.

The gains in both stocks rest largely on newly-signed, hyperscaler-linked supply contracts. A contract is a promise about the future. What the market is pricing is not what either company has shipped, but what it might ship — and the gap between those two things is where fortunes get made and lost. Sterlite's billion-dollar award letter is with an unnamed customer, which means investors are trusting management's characterization of it. The order book is real, but an order book is not revenue. Revenue is fiber that has been drawn, cabled, shipped, installed, and accepted.

The cost of this moment is that everyone can see it. When fiber was a glut industry, nobody wanted to build capacity. Now the returns are visible, capital will flood in, and the same oversupply that crushed both companies from 2022 to 2024 becomes a live risk again — just three or four years out, which is roughly how long it takes to build a fiber plant. The companies that survive the next glut will be the ones with long-term contracts that lock in pricing and volume through the down cycle. Sterlite's order book through FY29 is exactly that kind of insurance. HFCL's position is harder to read from the outside, which is itself the answer to the comparison: one company has told the market what it has locked in, and the other has told it less.

Where does AI genuinely enter? Not as a feature either company sells. Neither Sterlite nor HFCL trains models or rents compute. AI enters as a physical constraint — a rack that needs ten times the fiber, a cluster that stalls when the connections can't keep up. The companies are not AI companies. That's the larger arc. Every wave of computing has been bottlenecked by something physical — first transistors, then memory, then power, and now the wiring between chips. The companies that own the bottleneck get repriced violently, because the market spends years assuming the bottleneck is permanent and then discovers overnight that it moved. Sterlite and HFCL didn't become interesting because they changed. They became interesting because the thing they make stopped being a commodity and started being a constraint. The question for anyone holding either stock is not which company is better. It's how long the constraint lasts — and whether the contracts they've signed outlast it.
