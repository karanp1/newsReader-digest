[OpenAI] LegalOn halves Codex costs while maintaining development speed
  08 Oct 2026 | https://openai.com/index/legalon-halves-codex-costs
  LegalOn gave every engineer the most powerful model it had. That worked, right up until the invoice arrived. ¶ The company builds AI for legal work, and it had wired Codex into how its developers write software. GPT-5.5 in Fast mode, unlimited, across design, implementation and daily work. Then the math caught up. Unlimited use of a top model cannot survive an annual budget, and a blanket ban would have thrown away the gains. So the company did something less obvious than switching tools. It taught people to choose. ¶ An internal group, the AI-powered Development CoE, wrote model-selection guidelines and tested which model fit which job. Estimated daily costs fell about 65% against GPT-5.5, and development speed held, helped by running tasks in parallel. ¶ Here is the part worth sitting with. LegalOn's own engineering manager, Yuta Tokitake, says faster development is nearly a given now. What he cannot yet see is whether it produces anything a customer values. So the company is building a metric around each feature release, tying the customer value it delivers to the AI cost spent on it. Today you can price a task. You cannot price a feature. ¶ My view: cost control is the easy half of this problem, and most teams will stop there. The harder discipline is refusing to call cheaper AI a win until someone can name what the customer got.

Two-thirds keep discovering AI investments they already paid for somewhere else. And 78% say customer-facing channels still run on no shared data model, which means the same customer can look like three different people to three different products. ¶ My view: the orchestration layer will not be bought as software. [Open Claw Newsletter] Nadella Wants an AI Emergency Brake
  12 Oct 2026 | https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1009337/satya-nadella-says-we-should-assume-all-ai-models-are-compromised
  The most powerful buyer of AI in the world just asked for a kill switch. Satya Nadella, writing on X, says we can no longer treat advanced models as "nested black boxes" whose answers we simply accept or reject. He wants them contained, observed, and forced to leave what he calls tamper-proof human readable evidence. ¶ The sharpest line is about containment. Assume a model is compromised, he says, and contain it from the start. An authorized person should always be able to pause or shut down a model mid-task. He calls it an emergency brake, and adds that more advanced models will need more advanced containment, standardized across the industry. ¶ The rest of his list is familiar. Disclose incidents quickly. Submit to independent audits. Verify the data. Anyone who has watched AI policy for two years has heard versions of this. The brake is where he moves ahead of his peers. Most labs talk about alignment, which is a research problem. A brake is an operational one. It assumes the model will fail, and asks who holds the handle. ¶ That framing matters for anyone shipping AI products. Containment is an architecture decision, not a policy one. A system you can pause mid-task has to be built with checkpoints, state you can freeze, and an owner with the authority to stop it. Retrofitting that after launch is expensive. Building it in changes what the product can do. ¶ One tell: Nadella calls the technology "superintelligence" throughout. That word does work. It justifies the brake. It also flatters the thing being braked. ¶ My view: the emergency brake is the right ask, and the hardest part is not the technology. It is naming the person authorized to pull it, and giving them the nerve to do it while the model is still talking.

### Winning, differently: On the by-election results in West Bengal, Tamil Nadu and Puducherry
12 Oct 2026 | https://www.thehindu.com/opinion/editorial/winning-differently-on-the-by-election-results-in-west-bengal-tamil-nadu-and-puducherry/article71575161.ece

The by-election results in West Bengal, Tamil Nadu and Puducherry, declared on Friday, held few surprises. Barely five months after the April Assembly elections, the ruling party in each State/UT won all five bypolls. What varied was the scale of victory. In West Bengal, the BJP retained Nandigram and wrested Rejinagar , with strong vote shares of 76% and 63%. In Puducherry, the ruling All India N.R. Congress also registered an emphatic win — 61% of the votes — in Thattanchavady. Nandigram and Thattanchavady were vacated by the Chief Ministers, which partly explains the strong wins. But the contests in Tamil Nadu were closer. Dharapuram and Madurantakam fell vacant after their AIADMK MLAs resigned and defected to the ruling TVK , which renominated them. Both won their seats back with 37% and 40% of the vote, respectively, leading the DMK rival candidates by about eight percentage points. By-elections tend to favour the party in power, as voters prefer representatives with access to government. But the variance between West Bengal and Tamil Nadu reflects the nature of contest in two similarly sized States but with vastly different political cultures.

In West Bengal, Assembly victories have for decades worked as a winner-take-all mandate, which the ruling party extends to panchayat and by-elections. The pattern took shape during the Left Front’s long rule, became entrenched during the Trinamool Congress’s 15 years in power and is visible in the BJP’s tenure so far. In these contests, Opposition parties have performed far worse than in Assembly and Lok Sabha elections. In Tamil Nadu, however, a three-way contest now has kept politics competitive. The wins have helped the TVK improve its tally to 109 in the House with a sanctioned membership of 234, but the narrow margins, despite a no-holds-barred campaign by Chief Minister C. Joseph Vijay, suggest that the expected honeymoon effect did not fully materialise. The DMK, now the TVK’s principal rival, has shown that it still has a solid base despite its abrupt defeat in May. For the AIADMK, finishing third in the seats it won in May only deepens worries about its decline. Its leadership will now face sharper questions about whether it can hold on to its legislators. Four more AIADMK MLAs had joined the TVK and their seats will go to bypolls. This is a regressive turn in Tamil Nadu. MLAs are resigning barely months, sometimes days, into their terms to share in the loaves and fishes of office and the ruling party re-fields them. This defeats the spirit of the anti-defection law and overrides the Assembly election mandate. That the electorate rewarded these manoeuvres should worry anyone who is concerned about the quality of representation.

### Drought report: On southwest monsoon, drought in  Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh
12 Oct 2026 | https://www.thehindu.com/opinion/editorial/drought-report-on-southwest-monsoon-drought-in-karnataka-and-andhra-pradesh/article71575181.ece

A substantial drought seems to be inflicting Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh after the crucial southwest monsoon concluded with a significant deficiency in the most-affected regions. The national drought framework combines agricultural and hydrological indicators and ground-truthing studies, including before a distressed State can access additional support from the NDRF. In June-September, Karnataka reportedly suffered a close to 35% rainfall deficit . The government has since declared 217 of 240 taluks to be drought-hit with extensive damage to red gram, maize, cotton, and sugarcane cultivation. Andhra Pradesh experienced a shortfall of 53% in June-August and 388 of 688 mandals have been declared drought-hit . Horticulture crops over nearly 18,000 hectares are now at risk. Karnataka has also petitioned the Centre for ₹11,000 crore from the NDRF along with higher input subsidies. Andhra Pradesh has set aside ₹190 crore to protect horticultural crops and has directed drinking water conservation. The summer monsoon had its poorest showing since 2015 this year. There have been warnings of the effects of the strengthening El Niño, although an attribution study elucidating the strength of causal relationships in the present instance is pending.

In a previous era, drought declarations were beset by subjective claims, sometimes exaggerated to maximise state support. Since 2009, however, the Manual for Drought Management and updates to it and Finance Commission decisions have tightened the process. While States still have some incentives to expand their declarations, the Centre has also resisted releasing NDRF assistance before, including with Karnataka in 2024, while also increasing their own relief obligations. In addition to mitigating indebtedness among farmers and expanding access to relief beyond land ownership, the States also need more discipline vis-à-vis storing water and rationalising consumption. Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka have offered incentives and advisories, including to adopt micro-irrigation and to cultivate less water-intensive crops, but have not imposed binding limits. The Centre may also consider incentives to resist drought, even in the face of a deficient monsoon, because the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change has been warning of expanding drought-like conditions in South Asia, and broadening agricultural insurance. It must certainly not use the prospect of the more protracted, more imprudent river-interlinking project as an excuse to overlook pro-resilience aims such as sustainably using groundwater and choosing crops based on local rainfall and aquifers and availability of income support.

Ms. Pillay served on the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda and the International Criminal Court (ICC), and chaired the UN Independent International Commission of Inquiry on the Occupied Palestinian Territory. Born in Durban in 1941 to a family of Indian Tamil origin, Ms. Pillay grew up in apartheid South Africa, opened her own legal firm in the 1960s and defended anti-apartheid political prisoners and activists on the notorious Robben Island, including Nelson Mandela. ### List When the Business Is Predictable, and Stay Transparent After: Groww, Meesho Founders
12 Oct 2026 | https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/tech/newsletters/morning-dispatch/the-et-startup-awards-2026-special/articleshow/134880370.cms

Vidit Aatrey and Lalit Keshre listed their companies a month apart late last year. On a panel at the ET Startup Awards 2026, moderated by ETtech editor Samidha Sharma, they disagreed about why founders are rushing to the public markets earlier than before.

Aatrey sees a practical shift. In the US, small IPOs are rare. In India, both small and large listings happen. Founders look at that and ask a direct question: can I short-circuit the private fundraising and go public instead? Public markets, in this reading, are not a graduation ceremony. They are an alternative route.

Keshre put the question differently. Capital, he said, is available where capital is needed. The problem is valuation. At a certain stage, valuing an asset is very tough. In private markets, two parties decide. In public markets, a lot more parties do. Groww listed in November, and Meesho followed, two decade-old companies choosing the same door for different reasons.

Both agreed on the precondition. Startups should go public only when their businesses have achieved a degree of predictability. That word is doing heavy lifting. It means revenue you can forecast, costs you can explain, and a story you can repeat quarter after quarter to strangers.

Here is the tension. The private route rewards momentum. The public route punishes surprises. A founder who lists early trades the comfort of a small board for the scrutiny of thousands of shareholders who read every filing. The upside is capital that does not dilute on someone else's timeline. The downside is that every miss becomes a headline.

My view: predictability is the real IPO readiness test, not scale. The founders who understand that will list well, whenever they choose to.

### Deeptech Wants Government to Step Up as the First Customer
12 Oct 2026 | https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/tech/newsletters/morning-dispatch/the-et-startup-awards-2026-special/articleshow/134880370.cms

Kshitij Khandelwal makes sensors that go into space, and the supply chain is getting harder. Geopolitical tensions in West Asia have lifted demand for airborne sensors and related equipment, and with that demand came higher costs and longer waits. Pixxel, the spacetech company he cofounded and serves as CTO, feels that strain directly.

On a panel at the ET Startup Awards 2026 titled Building the Hard Stuff in India, founders laid out what they need. Dheemanth Nagaraj of Agrani Labs said Indian deeptech companies need the government to become an early customer and create markets for technologies built at home.

The ask is not charity. It is a market. Global incumbents sell to governments first, and that first customer absorbs the risk of a product that is not yet proven. It funds the iteration. It writes the reference. A startup selling to private buyers alone must prove itself twice, once on the technology and again on the balance sheet.

Space is the sharpest case. Components cross borders, and borders move. A supplier in one region can become unreachable in a week, and no amount of engineering talent fixes a part that cannot be shipped.

Archana Rajaram of River Law, Priya Narsimhan of Indus Appstore, IKEA India CEO Patrik Antoni and others filled out a room where the hard questions were about procurement, not code. Laina Emmanuel of BrainSight AI and Prashanth Prakash of Accel sat on the same panel, which says something about how the conversation has shifted. The bottleneck is not ideas.

My view: deeptech in India will be built or delayed by the first purchase order. The government does not need to pick winners. It needs to buy from its own.
