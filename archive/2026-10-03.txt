IT sector sees an uptick in fresher hiring after two years of slowdown

India’s $300 billion technology and

IT services industry is seeing a revival in fresher onboarding after nearly two years of subdued hiring.

Even so, entry-level recruitment remains below mid-level hiring as demand for experienced AI talent stays strong.

Number wise: Fresher hiring, which fell to a record low of around 110,000 in FY26, is expected to rise to 115,000-135,000 in the current fiscal year, according to recruitment firm Quess Group. That implies a 4.5-22.7% increase.

FY22: 380,000

FY23: 250,000

FY24: 155,000

FY25: 125,000

FY26: ~110,000

- Bitcoin headed 'in bullish direction' as investors bet on Fed pause: Chart of the Day
  03 Oct 2026 | https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/article/bitcoin-headed-in-bullish-direction-as-investors-bet-on-fed-pause-chart-of-the-day-120631623.html
  Bitcoin spent the first half of 2026 getting beaten up, and by Friday it was hovering near $85,000, having clawed back most of what it lost. That sentence contains the whole tension of the moment, because the thing that was supposed to hurt crypto — rising Treasury yields, the government's own borrowing costs creeping upward — didn't kill it. Bitcoin just closed its strongest quarter since 2024, up 43%, while the yield on short-term government debt kept climbing. That shouldn't happen if the old story about bitcoin were true. ¶ The old story went like this: bitcoin is a risk asset, and risk assets fall when safe assets start paying real money. A Treasury bond is a loan you make to the US government. The "yield" is the interest it pays you back. When that number goes up, parking cash in government debt gets more attractive, and speculative stuff — crypto, tech stocks, a friend's kombucha franchise — gets less attractive by comparison. For most of bitcoin's life, that relationship held. Yields up, bitcoin down. Simple. ¶ Except the relationship has been quietly breaking. Sean Farrell, who runs digital asset strategy at Fundstrat, pointed at a more specific number this week: the 2-year real yield, which is the interest on a two-year Treasury bond after subtracting inflation. Strip inflation out and you're measuring what you actually get for lending the government money — your true reward for playing it safe. That number has been falling. And if it keeps falling, the case for holding short-term government debt weakens, which is a polite way of saying the competition bitcoin faces for investor dollars gets less intimidating. Not because bitcoin got better. Because the alternative got worse. ¶ Here's where the story gets interesting, and where most people will get it wrong. The obvious read on Friday's move was that the Fed blinked. A weak jobs report landed — fewer Americans working than expected — and more than three-quarters of market participants now expect the central bank to hold rates steady at its October meeting rather than raise them. Lower rates mean cheaper money, cheaper money means speculation, speculation means bitcoin. That's the version that fits on a bumper sticker. ¶ But Farrell isn't making that argument. He's making a stranger one. Continued stress in sovereign bonds and credit markets — the plumbing underneath the entire global financial system — could still trigger a short-term drawdown in crypto. In other words, the thing that's supposedly helping bitcoin right now could also, at any moment, take it out back. His framing of that risk is the most revealing sentence in the whole story: "The 'glass half full' perspective is that the more stress that gets priced in without breaking BTC, the better the forward risk/reward becomes." Translation — every time the system creaks and bitcoin doesn't crack, the market learns something. It learns that bitcoin might be more durable than the models say. That's not a price forecast. That's a slow accumulation of evidence. ¶ Which is why the Citi revision matters more than it looks. Analyst Alex Saunders raised his base-case bitcoin target to $113,000 from $82,000. A base case is what an analyst thinks happens if nothing unusual occurs — it's the boring middle of the distribution, not the dream scenario. When the boring middle moves up 38% in one revision, the analyst isn't predicting a rally. He's admitting the old middle was wrong. ¶ Now the part nobody wants to say out loud: seasonality. Farrell notes October has historically been crypto's strongest month, with the highest median and average returns and roughly an 80% win rate. An 80% win rate over a handful of Octobers is not a law of nature. It's a pattern with a sample size small enough to fit in a spreadsheet cell. But patterns move markets precisely because enough people believe them, and right now enough people believe them. Seasonality is a self-fulfilling story with a calendar attached. ¶ So what actually changed this week? Not bitcoin's code. Not its adoption. Not a single line of the protocol. What changed is that two forces that used to push in the same direction — government bond yields and crypto prices — are now pushing in opposite ones, and the market is being forced to pick which story it believes. Either bitcoin is still a risk asset that will get dragged down the moment credit markets seize, or it's becoming something else, something that holds its ground while the bond market wobbles. Those two possibilities cannot both be true forever. ¶ The honest answer is that nobody knows yet, and the people closest to the trade are hedging in plain sight. Farrell's own note contains both the bullish case and the drawdown warning in the same paragraph. That's not indecision. That's what it looks like when a thesis is mid-transition — when the old model has stopped working but the new one hasn't been proven. Bitcoin at $85,000, up 43% in a quarter, with yields rising and a Fed that might hold steady, is a market that has stopped asking whether crypto is risky and started asking what, exactly, it's risky relative to. That question doesn't have a chart answer. It has a decade-long answer, and the decade isn't over.

- Bitget Hack Update: XRP, Bitcoin, and North Korean Link
  03 Oct 2026 | https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/crypto/articles/bitget-hack-xrp-bitcoin-north-130000092.html
  The first three hours are the tell. Twenty-three outbound transfers, moving $387 million across four different blockchains, and not one of them lands on an exchange where a compliance officer might freeze it. Ethereum takes 49.7 percent. The XRP Ledger takes 40.8 percent. Zcash, the privacy coin, absorbs 7.6 percent, Tron 1.8 percent. To anyone watching the raw ledger, this looks like chaos — money spraying in every direction. It isn't. It's a withdrawal, executed in the open, by people who understood exactly what the surveillance could and couldn't see. ¶ Here's the mechanism, because the mechanism is the whole story. When you steal tokens, you steal a liability. A stolen XRP sitting in a wallet is radioactive: the moment it touches a regulated exchange, that exchange's compliance team — the people whose job is to match incoming deposits against a list of known theft addresses — flags it, freezes it, and the money is gone. So the attackers don't sell. A sale requires a counterparty with a bank account and a name. Instead they deposit the XRP into a cross-chain liquidity protocol, which is a pool of funds that will swap you one asset on one blockchain for a different asset on another, no order book, no exchange, no identity check. What comes out the other side is Bitcoin, on a different network, in a wallet that has no visible connection to the XRP that went in. The tokens never touched a place that could ask who they belonged to. That's the trick. Not hacking an exchange twice — hacking one exchange and then laundering the proceeds through plumbing that was built to be trustless and is therefore also built to be blameless. ¶ Chainalysis matched the deposits to their payouts — tens of millions of dollars over roughly thirty-six hours — and the trail now ends at attacker-controlled Bitcoin addresses the firm monitors. Which is a strange kind of victory. They know where the money is. They just can't take it back. ¶ This is the third time this year the same crew has done this. On April 1, hackers drained $285 million from Drift Protocol, and Drift said the operation involved months of social engineering, including in-person meetings — actual humans, sitting across actual tables, building trust before pulling the plug. On April 18, a bridge exploit at KelpDAO took $292 million, and both TRM and LayerZero tied it to TraderTraitor, a unit affiliated with Lazarus, the North Korean state hacking operation that has been funding the regime through crypto theft for years. Those two hacks alone were 76 percent of all crypto hack losses through April. Add Bitget's $387 million and the 2026 haul crosses $1 billion. In 2025, the same actors stole over $2 billion. This is not a crime wave. It's a revenue stream, and it's growing. ¶ Bitget's CEO, Gracy Chen, didn't wait for the forensics. Shortly after the September 24 breach she pointed at North Korea, citing IP addresses that matched a DPRK group's known VPN choices. That's a small detail with a large implication: the attackers' operational security has a fingerprint. They reuse infrastructure. Which means the defenders aren't hunting ghosts — they're hunting an organization with habits, and habits can be anticipated. ¶ Which brings us to the part of this story that isn't about North Korea at all. Chainalysis said it built custom automations using its in-house AI to trace the cross-chain swaps, and that what would have taken more than twenty hours of manual bridge reconciliation — a human sitting at a screen, matching deposits on one chain to withdrawals on another, block by block — shrank to under ten minutes. Investigators still set the logic and reviewed the results. Read that sentence twice, because it's the honest version of how AI enters this story. The machine isn't deciding who the criminal is. It's doing the tedium: reconciling thousands of transactions across four ledgers that don't share a clock or a format, at a speed no analyst could match. The analyst still has to know what to look for. ¶ And that's the asymmetry that actually matters. The attackers used automation too — Chainalysis's own statement says North Korea is "increasingly using sophisticated automation to move and obscure stolen funds." Both sides are now running machines against each other, and the defender's machine has a constraint the attacker's doesn't: it has to be right, and it has to be defensible in a courtroom. The attacker's machine just has to be fast. Ten minutes versus twenty hours is a real victory, but it's a victory in a race where the other runner keeps changing shoes. ¶ Zoom out and the picture gets uncomfortable. Crypto's founding promise was that you wouldn't need to trust institutions — the code would enforce the rules, and no bank could freeze you out. That promise is precisely what makes it the ideal treasury for a sanctioned state. Cross-chain bridges exist because blockchains can't talk to each other, and someone has to move value between them; that someone is a liquidity pool with no compliance department, because adding one would defeat the point. Every piece of infrastructure that makes crypto useful to a dissident in a country with frozen banking is the same infrastructure that makes it useful to Lazarus. You cannot sever one without severing the other. ¶ So the industry is left doing forensics after the fact, publishing reports, monitoring addresses it cannot seize, and building faster tracing tools to keep pace with faster laundering. Bitget's $387 million is now the largest crypto hack of 2026 and pushed September losses up 462 percent. The number will be surpassed. The question isn't whether the next bridge gets drained — it's whether the tracing gets fast enough to matter before the money finishes moving. Ten minutes is a start. The attackers only needed thirty-six hours.

- Wall Street on the brink of bearish majority after gold’s post-payrolls slide, Main Street abandons bullish bias as metals test support
  02 Oct 2026 | https://www.kitco.com/news/article/2026-10-02/wall-street-brink-bearish-majority-after-golds-post-payrolls-slide-main
  The strange thing about gold is that it’s supposed to be the asset you reach for when everything else is wobbling. And yet here was a week where the wobbles were everywhere — a war premium baked into oil, a jobs report that missed so badly it looked like a typo — and gold spent most of it sliding. It opened Sunday night at $4,277.90 an ounce. By Friday it had touched $4,110.95, and it closed just fifteen dollars off the session low. The metal that exists to absorb fear refused to absorb any. ¶ To understand why, you have to understand what gold is actually competing against, and it isn’t stocks. Gold pays you nothing. It sits in a vault and glitters. That’s the whole proposition. Which means its price is really a measure of what you’re giving up by holding it — and this week, what you were giving up got more expensive. Long-term Treasury yields, the interest the U.S. government pays to borrow your money for a decade, climbed. When a risk-free bond starts paying real money, the cost of parking capital in a metal that pays zero goes up. Add a firmer dollar — and gold, priced in dollars, gets more expensive for anyone holding yen or euros — and you have two headwinds blowing at once. Normally that’s enough to knock gold down. This week it got a third: higher oil, driven by the U.S.-Iran conflict, which keeps inflation anxiety alive, which keeps the Fed’s finger near the rate-hike button. ¶ That last part is the knot. A rate hike is the Fed raising the price of borrowing money, and it’s the bluntest anti-inflation tool there is. Markets spent the week pricing in the chance of another one in October. So the picture for gold was: a competing asset paying more, a currency making it dearer abroad, and a central bank that might tighten into an oil shock. Sellers took control Monday and mostly never let go. ¶ Then Friday arrived, and the jobs report came in at 29,000 new jobs. For context, the U.S. economy needs something in the neighborhood of 100,000 a month just to keep up with population growth. The unemployment rate ticked up to 4.2%, and July and August were revised lower — meaning the recent past was weaker than we thought. This is the kind of number that should have been rocket fuel for gold. It cut the odds of an October hike. The dollar fell. Yields fell. And gold did rally — briefly. Then it failed to hold above $4,200 and slid hard into the weekend. ¶ Sean Lusk, who runs commercial hedging at Walsh Trading, sounded like a man watching a law of physics break. “I thought, with crude being down and equities being up and the dollar under pressure, we’d be up,” he said. “Initial reaction was positive, but now we’re negative, and you just can’t get any traction.” He called it illogical. He’s right that it’s illogical, and he’s also describing something real: when an asset stops responding to its own good news, that’s information. It tells you the marginal buyer has already bought. Lusk’s read is that gold “just ran its course for a couple of years for now.” Not turning over, exactly. Just unable to sustain a rally. “You want to bottom-feed down here, and it looks okay for a day or two, and then boom: You react bearishly to bullish news.” ¶ The survey data tells the same story from the crowd’s side. Kitco’s weekly poll of thirteen analysts found nearly half of Wall Street turning bearish — only three expected gains, six expected declines. And Main Street, the retail investors who had been bullish since the end of July, finally gave up that bias. That matters more than it sounds. Retail sentiment in gold is a momentum indicator as much as a forecast; when the true believers fold, you’re usually closer to a bottom than a top, but you’re also watching the last source of bid support step away. ¶ Not everyone reads it as the end. Marc Chandler of Bannockburn Global Forex pointed to signals from Fed leadership — Jefferson on the Board, Williams at the New York Fed — suggesting more patience than the market assumed, and noted gold recaptured $4,200 before the weekend. “A move above the $4,280-$4,300 area would boost confidence that a low is in place.” Adam Button at investingLive went neutral, waiting for an October buying opportunity before what he calls the strong seasonals of November through January. Adrian Day, who runs an asset management firm, expects gold a little lower but “not for long,” and framed the real driver as fiscal: an end to the war would let the dollar resume falling “amidst impossible U.S. deficits,” while a Fed pause would give gold relief. Rich Checkan at Asset Strategies International was one of the three bulls, arguing the weak jobs number plus Williams’ comment about no urgency to hike should let gold “soar a little.” ¶ Here’s the part worth sitting with. Every one of those bulls is making a bet about the Fed, the dollar, or the calendar. None of them is making a bet about gold being scarce, or useful, or newly desired. Gold’s case this week wasn’t about gold at all — it was about whether the thing you give up to hold it gets cheaper. And the honest answer from the tape is that right now, with equities melting up and yields elevated, the opportunity cost is still winning. Lusk put it plainly: “All the love is in equities right now, and that’s been it.” ¶ So watch the $4,280 to $4,300 band. If gold clears it, the October seasonal trade has legs and the bulls who stayed patient get paid. If it can’t, then the more interesting question isn’t where gold goes next — it’s what it means that the world’s oldest fear gauge spent a week full of fear and couldn’t find a bid. Either the fear is already priced in, or the market has decided that in a world of 5% yields, the safest thing to own isn’t a metal at all. That’s a bigger story than one week’s range.

- KSB stock trades 1.55 percent below its prior close
  02 Oct 2026 | https://www.ad-hoc-news.de/boerse/news/corporate-news/ksb-stock-trades-1-55-percent-below-its-prior-close/70216467
  A pump is a boring thing until it stops. Then it becomes the only thing. A municipal water authority, a chemical plant, a power station—each of them runs on machinery that nobody photographs and nobody tweets about, and each of them will pay almost anything to keep that machinery moving. KSB has been selling that insurance since 1871. On October 2, 2026, its shares traded at EUR 824.50, down 1.55 percent from the prior close of EUR 837.50, which is a rounding error in a year that has seen the stock as high as EUR 1,265.00 and as low as EUR 771.00. The number worth staring at is not the daily move. It is the gap between the order book and the bottom line. ¶ Order intake rose 8.80 percent in the first half of 2026. Sales revenue grew 0.40 percent. Read those two figures side by side and you have the whole story: customers are committing to more equipment than KSB is currently converting into revenue, and the revenue it does convert is arriving with less profit attached. Reported EBIT—earnings before interest and taxes, the line that tells you whether the core business makes money before financing and accounting noise—came in at EUR 98.00 million. Earnings after income tax: EUR 60.00 million. The company described the second quarter as materially stronger than the first, which is management's way of saying the year started slow and then found its footing. It reaffirmed the full-year outlook. That is not a company in trouble. That is a company being squeezed. ¶ The squeeze has a name and a number. KSB's material-cost ratio—the share of revenue eaten by the raw materials, components and bought-in parts that go into a pump or a valve—rose from 39.00 percent to more than 40.00 percent. One percentage point sounds like nothing until you apply it to a business with EUR 1.5 billion in market capitalization and thin industrial margins. Energy and logistics costs climbed alongside it. A pump is heavy, it is made of cast metal, and it moves across borders on ships and trucks. When freight rates rise and electricity bills rise and the price of steel rises, the manufacturer eats the difference until it can renegotiate contracts. KSB is now trying to renegotiate contracts. ¶ The response is a structural cost program worth up to EUR 30.00 million in fiscal 2026, of which more than EUR 10.00 million had already landed in the first half. Structural means it is not a hiring freeze or a travel ban. It means closing or consolidating something, or automating something, or moving something. It means a decision that is expensive this quarter and cheaper every quarter after. The company has not said which plants or which functions, because saying so would alarm the workforce of more than 16,800 people and the unions that represent them. What it has said is that the savings are real and arriving on schedule. The market's reaction on October 2 suggests investors are not yet convinced the savings will outrun the cost inflation. ¶ Here is where the story gets interesting, and where it connects to something larger than one German industrial company. KSB's business is organized around three segments: Pumps, Valves, and KSB SupremeServ. That last one is the tell. SupremeServ is the aftermarket—maintenance, spare parts, repairs, service contracts. It is the part of the business that does not depend on a customer deciding to build a new plant. It depends on a customer deciding that the plant they already have must not stop. Aftermarket revenue is recurring, higher-margin, and stickier than new equipment sales, because once a utility's maintenance schedule is built around KSB parts and KSB technicians, switching to a competitor means rewriting the schedule and retraining the crew. The company's own investor materials frame the model around energy efficiency, maintenance and critical industrial applications—water infrastructure, power generation. Those are not discretionary purchases. A city cannot decide to skip its water pumps this year. ¶ So the tension in the stock is not demand. Demand is fine. Order intake is up nearly nine percent. The tension is that the mix of that demand—and the cost of fulfilling it—has shifted against the company in the short term. New equipment orders carry lower margins than service contracts, and raw material costs hit new equipment hardest. If the order intake is weighted toward new-build projects, the profit arrives later and thinner. If it is weighted toward service, the profit arrives sooner and fatter. The earnings call did not break that down, which is why the stock sits where it sits. ¶ The analysts have not capitulated. Berenberg maintained a Buy rating with a EUR 1,300.00 target on July 23, 2026. Warburg Research maintained a Buy with a EUR 1,050.00 target on August 7. Both targets sit well above the October 2 price of EUR 824.50. Either the analysts are early, or the market is overreacting to a cost cycle that KSB's own cost program is designed to break. The 52-week range tells you the market has already repriced the company once this year, from EUR 1,265.00 down to EUR 771.00, and has since recovered some ground. This is a stock that has been through a full argument with itself and has not yet reached a verdict. ¶ The larger arc is this: the industrial companies that keep the physical world running are being repriced not by a technology shift but by an input-cost shift. The pumps and valves and service networks do not change because software got better. They change because the cost of the metal and the electricity and the shipping that go into them changes, and because the customers who depend on them will pay for reliability but will not pay infinitely for it. KSB's job in the next two quarters is to prove that a business built on 16,800 people and a century and a half of installed equipment can absorb a one-point rise in material costs without surrendering its margins. If it can, the gap between EUR 824.50 and EUR 1,300.00 closes. If it cannot, the stock is not cheap. It is early.
