Bitcoin Investors Who Bought Last Year's Rally Are Selling As Price Nears Their Break-Even, Glassnode Says

03 Oct 2026

Bitcoin buyers from last year's rally are selling at the fastest daily pace of 2025, according to on-chain analytics firm Glassnode, because the price has fallen back to what they paid. Glassnode grouped holders by purchase date and calculated each group's average cost basis, the price at which they break even. Buyers from six to 12 months ago paid about $89,000 on average, and buyers from one to two years ago paid about $97,000. With Bitcoin trading near $84,673, both groups are underwater. The six-to-12-month cohort needs roughly a 5% gain to break even, the older group nearly 15%.

That matters because underwater holders often wait for the price to return to their cost basis, then sell to exit without a loss. Each cost basis becomes a level where selling pressure builds, which helps explain why Bitcoin stalled below $85,000. Glassnode found the selling is concentrated in these 2025 buyers, while holders who bought during the decline are not moving coins. Analyst Ted Pillows says a weekly close above $87,500 is needed to confirm a rally; otherwise he sees a decent chance of a drop below $80,000. Watch that weekly close. If it fails, the cost-basis wall above will keep capping any bounce.

HAL to take full control of HATSOFF as Tejas Mk1A review nears

04 Oct 2026

Hindustan Aeronautics Limited will buy CAE Canada's 50 per cent stake in HATSOFF Helicopter Training Pvt. Ltd. for nil consideration, turning the simulator training joint venture into a wholly owned subsidiary. The transaction covers 3,84,04,205 equity shares of face value Rs 10 each and is expected to close within 60 days of signing the share transfer agreement, according to Source 3. HAL has administrative approval from the Ministry of Defence's Department of Defence Production and concurrence from the Department of Investment and Public Asset Management under the Ministry of Finance. Because HAL is a state-controlled defence enterprise and the deal involves a foreign partner's stake, that government sign-off was required. HAL disclosed the transaction as a related-party transaction under SEBI's listing regulations, since HATSOFF is already a 50 per cent-owned joint venture, and said it is not at arm's length.

The numbers at HATSOFF are small next to HAL. The training venture's turnover rose to Rs 80.95 crore in FY2025-26 from Rs 69.55 crore in FY2024-25 and Rs 66.49 crore in FY2023-24, Source 3 reports, a gain of about 16.4 per cent year on year. HAL's consolidated revenue from operations was Rs 33,088.82 crore in FY2025-26, so HATSOFF accounted for less than 0.3 per cent of group revenue. HATSOFF has authorised capital of Rs 76.84 crore and paid-up capital of Rs 76.80 crore. It was incorporated in January 2008 as a 50:50 venture between HAL and CAE Canada, and trains helicopter pilots for the Indian Air Force, Army, Navy, Coast Guard and Border Security Force, plus civil customers including Pawan Hans and Global Vectra. HAL says full control will secure management control and streamline decision-making, and tie simulator training more closely to helicopter deliveries, fleet support and customer service. That fits HAL's wider push into helicopter manufacturing and lifecycle support, including its greenfield helicopter project at Tumakuru.

The larger story for HAL is the Tejas Mk1A fighter, where a final review meeting with the Indian Air Force will be held after mid-October, Source 2 reports. The meeting was earlier slated for September and will take place only when HAL brings its final update on pending issues; that assessment will decide the induction timeline, a senior defence official told TNIE. Most integration work is done, with two to three minor issues left in the jet's radar. The Israeli EL/M-2052 AESA radar, an active electronically scanned array that steers its beam electronically rather than by moving parts, must mesh with the jet's weapon firing controls and its Angad electronic warfare suite. After that, the jet still needs final clearance from CEMILAC, the military airworthiness regulator. The first batch will go to Sulur in Tamil Nadu, home of the IAF's first Tejas Mk1 squadron, and the first full Mk1A squadron will be raised at Nal in Rajasthan's Bikaner district, near the western front with Pakistan. The IAF expects to induct the first jets within this financial year.

HAL chairman Ravi K said last month that 27 Mk1A airframes were ready and 10 engines had been received from GE Aerospace, with one or two issues in the first batch needing more testing; he put the programme at 90-95 per cent complete. HAL's own target is to deliver the first squadron of 16-18 jets by March 2027. Engine supply from GE has been the other choke point. GE shipped three F404 engines in August, taking the total to 10. Former HAL chief D K Sunil said GE was committed to supplying 20 engines between June and December this year. HAL has imposed liquidated damages, a contractual penalty for late delivery, on GE, and the defence ministry has warned that HAL itself could face liquidated damages for missing its own schedules.

The delivery record is stark. Under the Rs 48,000 crore contract signed in February 2021 for 83 Tejas Mk1A jets, deliveries were to begin in February 2024. Not one jet has been delivered so far. The defence ministry signed a second contract for 97 more jets in September last year worth Rs 62,370 crore, taking the total to 180, and HAL signed a follow-on engine deal with GE for those aircraft. The shortfall matters because the IAF is down to 29 fighter squadrons, each with 16-18 jets, after retiring its MiG-21s, against a requirement of at least 42 to handle a threat from China and Pakistan together.

On the transport aircraft tender, Lockheed Martin says it would expand production in India if it wins the bid for 60 medium transport aircraft, valued at USD 10.5 billion, issued in August. Five Indian companies are in the running: Tata Advanced Systems Limited, Mahindra Group, Adani Defence & Aerospace, HAL and Reliance Defence, and each is expected to bring in a global aircraft maker. TASL has partnered with Lockheed Martin; Mahindra has tied up with Brazil's Embraer. Dan Tenney, Lockheed's senior vice president for global business development and strategy, said the company's existing work with Tata on C-130J components and empennages, the tail section of the aircraft, would be expanded in the current bid. Lockheed's proposal includes 12 aircraft in flyaway condition and 48 manufactured in India, plus an offer to upgrade the IAF's 12 existing C-130Js to the same advanced configuration, which could give the force a common fleet of 72 aircraft. The current C-130J fleet has flown more than 58,000 hours, used for high-altitude operations in Ladakh, Nepal flood relief and the 2023 evacuation from Sudan.

What remains unconfirmed is the timing on both fronts. The Tejas review has no fixed date beyond "after mid-October", and the radar fixes, CEMILAC clearance and GE's engine deliveries all sit outside HAL's direct control. Whether Lockheed's India production plan becomes real depends on a bid outcome that has not been decided, and HAL is one of five contenders rather than the front-runner named in the reporting. The HATSOFF deal is approved but not yet closed; the 60-day window is the milestone to watch.

The risks are concentrated in execution. HAL is already exposed to penalties on Tejas if it misses schedules, and its supplier GE has been late. A further slip would delay a squadron the IAF needs and keep the fighter gap open. The transport tender is competitive and capital-intensive, and losing it would leave HAL without a role in a USD 10.5 billion programme. HATSOFF is too small to move group earnings, and buying a partner out for nil consideration raises governance questions that the related-party and non-arm's-length labels flag, even with government approval.

My view: the HATSOFF purchase is a minor housekeeping step that adds a recurring training service to HAL's helicopter business but changes little financially. The Tejas review is the item that matters, because 180 jets worth more than Rs 1.1 lakh crore across two contracts are already sold and the company's problem is delivering them, not winning orders.

Next, watch the date and outcome of the IAF-HAL review, any CEMILAC clearance, GE's engine shipments against the 20-unit commitment for June to December, and the closing of the HATSOFF share transfer within 60 days. On the transport tender, watch which global partner pairs with which Indian company and when the 60-aircraft contract is awarded.

Info Edge Ventures co-leads $15 million round in interior materials startup Gravity

03 Oct 2026

Info Edge Ventures has co-led a $15 million funding round in Gravity, a Bengaluru-based startup that supplies materials for home interiors. The round was co-led with 3one4 Capital, with Alteria Capital, Genesia Ventures and a group of angel investors also participating. Gravity raised the money as a mix of equity and debt, though it did not disclose the split between the two. The news was reported on 3 October 2026.

Info Edge Ventures is the venture capital arm of Info Edge, the listed Indian company that owns the job portal Naukri and stakes in several internet businesses. Info Edge is the parent that most readers of this briefing will know as Naukri's owner. The $15 million round is a small cheque by Info Edge's standards, but it is a fresh deployment of capital into a private company, and it tells you where the venture arm is putting money.

Gravity was founded by Saurabh Jain and Lalit Mittal, both former executives at Livspace, an Indian home interiors and renovation company. Jain was CEO for India at Livspace, where he helped scale the India business to an annualised revenue run rate of around Rs 1,500 crore. Mittal was chief business officer for India at Livspace and led its expansion across 30 cities. Earlier in his career, Jain co-founded DezignUp, a design community and marketplace that Livspace acquired in March 2015, after which he and other members of its core team joined Livspace.

Gravity describes itself as a platform for India's interior materials sector. It is initially focused on kitchens and wardrobes, bringing specialist businesses onto shared technology, distribution, procurement, key-account management and operating infrastructure. Its customers are designers, architects, modular showrooms and design-and-build studios, the people who select and procure materials for interior projects. The company says it wants to simplify a supply chain that remains fragmented across dealers, distributors and suppliers that each specialise in individual product categories. The platform is meant to improve availability, fulfilment and pricing visibility for those customers.

Gravity said the businesses in its initial portfolio have a combined revenue base running into several hundred crore rupees and are EBITDA positive. EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation, a rough measure of operating profit. The company did not name the businesses in that portfolio, did not give a precise revenue figure, and did not say how many businesses are included. Those are gaps a reader should note.

The fresh capital will be used to strengthen technology, distribution and key-account infrastructure, expand into new categories, build the brand and support working capital as the company scales. Working capital is the cash a business needs for day-to-day operations, such as holding inventory and paying suppliers before customers pay. Gravity plans to expand into doors and windows, lighting, wall surfaces and home automation. It intends to use common technology, distribution and operating infrastructure across these categories rather than build separate systems for each business.

Why is this happening now? India's interior materials market is large and split among many dealers, distributors and category-specific suppliers. A platform that aggregates demand from designers and showrooms and offers shared back-end infrastructure can, in theory, get better prices from suppliers and better availability for customers. Gravity's founders have run a scaled interiors business before, which is the main reason investors are backing them at this stage. The venture arm of Info Edge has a history of backing consumer internet and marketplace businesses, and this fits that pattern.

What is still unknown is substantial. Gravity has not disclosed the equity-debt split, so the true cost of the capital and the dilution taken by existing shareholders are unclear. It has not named the businesses in its portfolio, so the claim of several hundred crore rupees in combined revenue and positive EBITDA cannot be checked. It has not said how many categories it will enter first or on what timeline. It has not said how the platform makes money, whether through commissions, subscriptions, or margin on materials sold. And it has not said how much of the $15 million is new money versus a structured debt facility from Alteria Capital, a specialist in venture debt.

For Info Edge, the risks here are the usual ones for a venture bet. The investment is small relative to the parent's overall business, so a poor outcome would not move the listed company's earnings much. But the interior materials supply chain is hard to change. Dealers and distributors have long-standing relationships, credit terms and local logistics. A platform that tries to insert itself between suppliers and buyers has to offer a clear reason for both sides to switch, and it has to do so across categories with different economics. Kitchens and wardrobes are project-based and involve measurement, customisation and installation. Lighting and home automation are more standardised but have their own specialist channels. Running all of them on shared infrastructure is the bet, and it is not yet proven.

There is also the question of competition. Livspace, where both founders worked, operates in the same broad space and has scale. Other players in building materials and home improvement have raised money and built distribution. Gravity's pitch is that it is a platform for materials rather than a consumer interiors brand, which puts it in a different lane, but the customers overlap.

What to watch next: whether Gravity names the businesses in its portfolio and gives a precise revenue figure; whether it discloses the equity-debt split; which new category it enters first and how quickly; and whether Info Edge Ventures participates in follow-on rounds. For the listed parent, the more important numbers remain those from Naukri and its other holdings, which report separately. This deal is a signal about where the venture arm sees opportunity, not a change to the earnings picture.

My view: this is a small, early-stage bet for Info Edge and it will not change the company's financial results in any meaningful way in the near term. Its value to a shareholder is as a read on the venture arm's judgment and on the founders' ability to build a platform business in a fragmented market, and that will take several years to judge.

The eternal complement

04 Oct 2026

The essay argues that human progress has always depended less on raw genius than on the machinery and bureaucracy needed to turn ideas into reality, and that AI is about to change which of those two things is scarce. The authors point to the James Webb Space Telescope: Galileo widened our view of the universe with two lenses and a tube, while Webb required eighteen mirror segments engineered to fifty nanometer precision, three hundred organizations across fourteen countries, and ten billion dollars. That gap is the point. Research productivity has been falling even as spending rises. Sustaining Moore's law now takes more than eighteen times as many researchers as it did in the early 1970s, and economy-wide research productivity fell by a factor of forty-one while research effort rose twenty-three-fold. The technician workforce is growing twice as fast as the scientists.

The authors call the unglamorous capacity to execute "institutional intelligence," and treat it as a complement to frontier intelligence: more of one raises the value of the other. Their bet is that AI will spend most of its time on the execution side, writing code, searching literature, turning sketches into prototypes. That would let one person pursue projects that once needed a whole organization, and push creative work outside the institutions that currently gatekeep it. The thing to watch is whether that produces a wave of independent work or just a new set of bottlenecks. My view: the essay is right that execution, not ideas, is the binding constraint, and that is exactly why the incumbents with the biggest bureaucracies stand to gain most.

The news

IRDAI released a consultation paper titled 'Recalibrating Economics of Insurance Distribution' proposing commission caps and lower expense limits for insurers. For life policies of 10 years or more, agent commissions would be capped at 25% in year one and distribution entities at 20%. General insurers would see expense limits cut from 30% of gross written premium to 20% of domestic GDPI over five years. PB Fintech fell about 34% on 24 September and continued falling, hitting a 52-week low of Rs 965 on 1 October. Bernstein cut its target price to Rs 1,085 from Rs 2,310. Stakeholder comments are due by 25 October 2026.

Jargon explained
Expenses of Management (EoM)
The total cost an insurer spends on running the business, including commissions to agents and distributors. IRDAI wants to cap this to lower the cost of insurance for policyholders.
Gross Written Premium (GWP)
The total premium an insurer collects from all policies before deducting reinsurance or other costs. It is a measure of business volume.
Gross Direct Premium Income (GDPI)
Premium collected from direct customers, excluding reinsurance assumed from other insurers. IRDAI proposes linking expense limits to this measure instead of GWP.
POSP (Point of Sales Person)
A person authorised to sell simple insurance products like motor or travel policies. PB Fintech uses POSPs in its distribution network, and the proposed rules could affect this business.
