IT sector sees an uptick in fresher hiring after two years of slowdown

India’s $300 billion technology and

IT services industry is seeing a revival in fresher onboarding after nearly two years of subdued hiring.

Even so, entry-level recruitment remains below mid-level hiring as demand for experienced AI talent stays strong.

Number wise: Fresher hiring, which fell to a record low of around 110,000 in FY26, is expected to rise to 115,000-135,000 in the current fiscal year, according to recruitment firm Quess Group. That implies a 4.5-22.7% increase.

The decline had been steady:

FY22: 380,000 FY23: 250,000

FY24: 155,000 FY25: 125,000

FY26: ~110,000 A rebound began in the June quarter, with TCS, Infosys and HCLTech together recruiting about 19,000 entry-level staff, Nasscom said in its September quarter report covering 29 IT services firms.

Higher bar for freshers: Experts told us the bar for entry-level talent has risen. Firms increasingly want specialised capabilities in AI, cloud, data and cybersecurity to improve revenue visibility, meet client demands and ensure deployment readiness with limited ramp-up time.

Bitcoin Investors Who Bought Last Year's Rally Are Selling As Price Nears Their Break-Even, Glassnode Says

03 Oct 2026

Bitcoin buyers from last year's rally are selling at the fastest daily pace of 2025, according to on-chain analytics firm Glassnode, because the price has fallen back to what they paid. Glassnode grouped holders by purchase date and calculated each group's average cost basis, the price at which they break even. Buyers from six to 12 months ago paid about $89,000 on average, and buyers from one to two years ago paid about $97,000. With Bitcoin trading near $84,673, both groups are underwater. The six-to-12-month cohort needs roughly a 5% gain to break even, the older group nearly 15%.

That matters because underwater holders often wait for the price to return to their cost basis, then sell to exit without a loss. Each cost basis becomes a level where selling pressure builds, which helps explain why Bitcoin stalled below $85,000. Glassnode found the selling is concentrated in these 2025 buyers, while holders who bought during the decline are not moving coins. Analyst Ted Pillows says a weekly close above $87,500 is needed to confirm a rally; otherwise he sees a decent chance of a drop below $80,000. Watch that weekly close. If it fails, the cost-basis wall above will keep capping any bounce.

HAL to buy out CAE's stake in HATSOFF as Tejas Mk1A review nears

04 Oct 2026

Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL) will acquire CAE Canada's 50 per cent stake in HATSOFF Helicopter Training Pvt. Ltd. for nil consideration, the company said on October 4, 2026. The deal covers 3,84,04,205 equity shares of face value Rs 10 each and turns the 50:50 joint venture, incorporated in January 2008, into a wholly owned HAL subsidiary. HAL expects to close the share transfer within about 60 days of signing. The Ministry of Defence's Department of Defence Production has given administrative approval, with concurrence from the Department of Investment and Public Asset Management under the Ministry of Finance. HAL calls the deal a related-party transaction under SEBI listing rules and says it is not at arm's length, because HATSOFF is already half-owned by HAL.

HATSOFF trains helicopter pilots on simulators for the Indian Air Force, Army, Navy, Coast Guard and Border Security Force, plus civil customers including Pawan Hans and Global Vectra. Its turnover rose to Rs 80.95 crore in FY2025-26 from Rs 69.55 crore in FY2024-25 and Rs 66.49 crore in FY2023-24, about 16.4 per cent growth year on year, according to the article. That is less than 0.3 per cent of HAL's consolidated revenue from operations of Rs 33,088.82 crore in FY2025-26. HAL says the purchase secures full management control and streamlines decision-making, and it fits the company's push into helicopter manufacturing, maintenance, repair, overhaul and lifecycle support, including its greenfield helicopter project at Tumakuru.

The larger story for HAL is the Tejas Mk1A fighter, where a final review meeting with the Indian Air Force will be held after mid-October, a top defence official told TNIE. The review had been slated for September. Two or three minor issues remain with the Israeli EL/M-2052 AESA radar, which must mesh with the jet's weapon firing controls and its Angad electronic warfare suite. After that, the military airworthiness regulator CEMILAC must give final clearance. The first batch will go to Sulur in Tamil Nadu, home of the IAF's first Tejas Mk1 squadron, the Flying Daggers, and the first full Mk1A squadron will be raised at Nal in Rajasthan's Bikaner district, near the Pakistan border. The IAF expects to induct the first jets within this financial year.

HAL chairman Ravi K said last month that 27 Mk1A airframes were ready and 10 engines had been received from GE Aerospace, with one or two issues in the first batch needing more testing. He put the programme at 90-95 per cent complete. HAL's own target is to deliver the first squadron of 16-18 jets by March 2027. Engine supply is the other bottleneck: GE shipped three F404 engines in August, taking the total to 10. Former HAL chief D K Sunil said GE was committed to supplying 20 engines between June and December this year. HAL has imposed liquidated damages on GE for the delays, and the defence ministry has warned that HAL itself could face liquidated damages for missing its own delivery schedules.

The delays are large. Under the Rs 48,000 crore contract signed in February 2021 for 83 Tejas Mk1A jets, deliveries were to begin in February 2024. Not one jet has been delivered. The ministry then signed another Rs 62,370 crore contract for 97 more jets in September last year, taking the total to 180, and HAL signed a follow-on engine deal with GE for them. The IAF is down to 29 fighter squadrons, each with 16-18 jets, after retiring its MiG-21s, against a requirement of at least 42 to handle the combined threat from China and Pakistan.

On the transport side, Lockheed Martin is bidding for India's tender, issued in August, for 60 medium transport aircraft at a cost of USD 10.5 billion. Five Indian companies are in the fray: Tata Advanced Systems Limited (TASL), Mahindra Group, Adani Defence & Aerospace, HAL and Reliance Defence, each expected to bring in a global aircraft maker. TASL has partnered with Lockheed Martin; Mahindra has joined Brazil's Embraer. Dan Tenney, Lockheed Martin's senior vice president for global business development and strategy, said the company's existing C-130J component and empennage work with Tata would be expanded if the bid succeeds. Lockheed's proposal includes 12 aircraft in flyaway condition and 48 built in India, plus an upgrade of the IAF's 12 existing C-130Js to the same advanced configuration, which could give the force a common fleet of 72 aircraft. The current C-130J fleet has flown more than 58,000 hours, used for high-altitude operations in Ladakh, Nepal flood relief and the 2023 evacuation from Sudan.

What is still unconfirmed: the exact date of the Tejas review, whether the radar issues will be resolved without further slippage, and whether CEMILAC clearance follows quickly. HAL has not given a delivery date for the first Mk1A beyond the March 2027 squadron target. The HATSOFF deal is agreed but not closed, and the 60-day timeline is tentative. On the transport tender, no winner has been named, and Lockheed's expansion plans are conditional on winning.

The risks are concentrated in execution. Tejas Mk1A deliveries have slipped by more than two years against the February 2024 start, and the engine supply from GE remains behind schedule despite liquidated damages. Every further delay weakens the IAF's squadron count and raises the chance of penalties against HAL. The HATSOFF purchase is tiny in revenue terms and is a related-party deal at nil consideration, so it will not move group earnings; its value is control over pilot training as HAL sells and supports more helicopters. The medium transport aircraft bid is a contest HAL is in but does not control, and losing it would leave the expansion to a rival consortium.

What to watch next: the date and outcome of the IAF-HAL review after mid-October, CEMILAC's clearance, and any new GE engine shipments. Watch whether HAL confirms the first Mk1A delivery this financial year. Watch the 60-day HATSOFF share transfer. And watch the medium transport aircraft tender for the winning pair of an Indian company and a global manufacturer.

My view: the HATSOFF buyout is a small housekeeping move that adds control, not revenue. The Tejas Mk1A timeline is the material item, because 180 jets worth about Rs 1.1 lakh crore in total contract value sit behind a radar integration issue and a slow engine supplier, and that is what will decide HAL's earnings and the IAF's squadron strength over the next few years.

The anchor book on October 19 will be the first major test.

My view: the IPO is material because it separates Jio Platforms from Reliance Industries and gives public investors direct exposure to a business that is still primarily telecom but is building digital and technology assets. The valuation premium to Airtel will depend on whether investors accept the digital ecosystem as a separate growth engine.

What to watch next: the RHP filing on October 15 or 16, the anchor book on October 19, the public issue from October 21 to 23, and the listing on October 28. The final price band and valuation will provide the clearest signal of how the market values Jio Platforms against its telecom peer.

The eternal complement

04 Oct 2026

The essay argues that human progress has always depended less on raw genius than on the machinery and bureaucracy needed to turn ideas into reality, and that AI is about to change which of those two things is scarce. The authors point to the James Webb Space Telescope: Galileo widened our view of the universe with two lenses and a tube, while Webb required eighteen mirror segments engineered to fifty nanometer precision, three hundred organizations across fourteen countries, and ten billion dollars. That gap is the point. Research productivity has been falling even as spending rises. Sustaining Moore's law now takes more than eighteen times as many researchers as it did in the early 1970s, and economy-wide research productivity fell by a factor of forty-one while research effort rose twenty-three-fold. The technician workforce is growing twice as fast as the scientists.

The authors call the unglamorous capacity to execute "institutional intelligence," and treat it as a complement to frontier intelligence: more of one raises the value of the other. Their bet is that AI will spend most of its time on the execution side, writing code, searching literature, turning sketches into prototypes. That would let one person pursue projects that once needed a whole organization, and push creative work outside the institutions that currently gatekeep it. The thing to watch is whether that produces a wave of independent work or just a new set of bottlenecks. My view: the essay is right that execution, not ideas, is the binding constraint, and that is exactly why the incumbents with the biggest bureaucracies stand to gain most.
