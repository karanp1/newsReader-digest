No need to slow AI deployment if technology clears safety test: ElevenLabs CEO

Mati Staniszewski, cofounder and CEO, ElevenLabs. (Photo by N Narasimha Murthy)

does not support an industry-wide slowdown in AI development, cofounder and CEO Mati Staniszewski told ET in an interview. He said companies should slow deployment where technology is found to be “unsafe,” but move ahead where it has been “adequately tested.”

Analysis: Why NASA wants to build a nuclear reactor on the moon

07 Oct 2026

Bitcoin falls nearly $2,000 in 20 minutes as $400 million in longs get liquidated

07 Oct 2026

Bitcoin fell nearly $2,000 in about 20 minutes on October 6, 2026, dropping more than 5% intraday from roughly $71,765 to $67,895, its lowest level since April. Around $394 million in positions were liquidated within a single hour, and $384 million of that came from longs, traders betting prices would rise. Across 24 hours, liquidations reached about $1.02 billion.

The mechanism is worth understanding. In crypto derivatives, a liquidation happens when a leveraged trader's collateral can no longer cover losses, so the exchange automatically closes the position by selling into the market. Those forced sales push prices down, which triggers more liquidations. Bitcoin absorbed about $209 million of the damage, Ethereum $87 million, Solana $27 million and XRP $11 million.

The trigger was a technical breakdown below key on-chain support levels, the price zones where buyers had previously defended the market. Strategy, the largest corporate Bitcoin holder, sold 32 BTC worth about $2.5 million to fund dividends, and broader pressure came from capital rotating into AI equities, strong labor data, rising energy prices and fading hopes for near-term rate cuts. The crowded long positioning was the real vulnerability. Watch whether Bitcoin reclaims the support it lost, or whether those former floors become ceilings.

HAL opens second Shakti engine line, lifts AL-31FP output to 50 a year

06 Oct 2026

Hindustan Aeronautics marked its 2,000th aero-engine on Tuesday at its Koraput division in Odisha, and used the event to announce two capacity expansions. Defence Minister Rajnath Singh inaugurated a second production line for the Shakti helicopter engine, built at a cost of ₹218 crore. The line will be able to make up to 100 Shakti engines a year once fully established by 2028-29, according to the government. Separately, HAL will raise annual output of the AL-31FP engine, which powers the Su-30MKI fighter, from 30 to 50 engines at an investment of ₹335 crore.

The 2,000th engine delivered was an AL-31FP. Koraput has now overhauled more than 9,000 engines, and the division dates to 1964. The Shakti engine is co-developed with France's Safran and powers the Dhruv Advanced Light Helicopter, its armed Rudra variant and the Prachand Light Combat Helicopter. HAL currently builds 80 Ardiden 1H1 Shakti engines a year at its Bengaluru facility, so the new Koraput line takes group capacity to 180 engines a year. Each Prachand carries two Shakti engines.

The order book behind the helicopter line is concrete. The armed forces have ordered 156 Prachands under contracts worth ₹62,700 crore signed in March 2025, with 66 for the Air Force and 90 for the Army. ICICI Securities, a brokerage, lists HAL's existing helicopter backlog as 25 Dhruv ALH, 12 Light Utility Helicopters, 156 Prachand LCH and 10 Dhruv NG ordered by Pawan Hans. A further 110 LUH and 95 LCH are planned but contracts have not been placed. The brokerage also notes HAL plans roughly ₹12,000 crore of investment in manufacturing infrastructure by 2030 across the LCA, HTT-40 and LCH programmes.

On the fighter side, the AL-31FP expansion is tied to a September 2024 Ministry of Defence contract worth over ₹26,000 crore for 240 engines for the Su-30MKI fleet. HAL was originally to execute that order over eight years. It has delivered 63 engines so far, against a target of 60 by the end of the second year, and the government says the first engine came ahead of schedule despite global supply-chain and raw-material problems. HAL estimates the Air Force will need around 900 engines over the Su-30 fleet's lifetime. The Air Force operates 260 Su-30s and will receive 12 more to replace aircraft lost in accidents. Engines are built at Koraput from the raw-material stage under technology transfer from Russia, with only some spares, forgings and castings imported.

The Tejas Mk1A programme remains the open question. It is running behind schedule, partly because US firm GE Aerospace has been slow to supply F404-IN20 engines. HAL has received 10 engines, according to Jefferies, a brokerage. HAL's chairman and managing director, Ravi Kota, said in a media interview after completing the Tejas Mk1 trainer contract on September 18, 2026 that he expects to deliver Tejas Mk1A aircraft this year. Jefferies reads the 10 engines as supporting delivery of 10 aircraft this year, against its own assumption of five, and calls the Tejas Mk1A 43 per cent of HAL's order book. The brokerage has a Buy rating and a ₹6,800 target, and says the stock de-rated on Mk1A delays since calendar 2024. It adds that the Air Force accepting even one aircraft would lift confidence in HAL's execution. HAL is also negotiating with GE Aerospace on joint production of F414 engines in India for the LCA Mk2.

Longer-dated propulsion plans were aired at the same event. Singh referred to a proposed investment of around ₹5,000 crore in an indigenous high-thrust aero-engine programme, including India's first indigenous high-thrust engine testing facility and indigenisation of raw materials with Mishra Dhatu Nigam. DRDO's Gas Turbine Research Establishment has proposed co-developing a 120kN-class engine with Safran for the Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft, a proposal worth nearly ₹60,000 crore that awaits clearance from the Cabinet Committee on Security. India is also in talks with Safran and Rolls Royce on domestic engine capability. The DRDO-developed Kaveri engine did not meet the thrust requirement for the Tejas, which is why the fighter depends on GE's F404.

Supply-chain context sits outside HAL. Hical Technologies, a Bengaluru precision manufacturer, opened a 60,000-square-foot plant in Devanahalli on the same day, making electromagnetic, electromechanical, hydraulic and control systems for commercial and defence programmes including the Rafale. Hical is developing components for HAL for the Tejas Mk2 and supplies Dassault Aviation, Hamilton Sundstrand, Goodrich, Eaton, Thales Solutions Asia and Moog Controls. It is also building a 160,000-square-foot aerospace facility in Hosur, where groundbreaking was completed in July 2026.

What is unconfirmed: the exact delivery schedule for the 240 AL-31FP engines now that capacity rises, and whether the Shakti line reaches its 100-engine annual rate on time by 2028-29. The 110 LUH and 95 LCH orders remain unplaced. The AMCA engine proposal has no clearance date. The Tejas Mk1A delivery count for this year rests on the chairman's stated confidence and 10 engines received, not on a signed-off schedule.

The risks are execution and dependence. Helicopter revenue depends on Shakti engine availability, which the new line addresses but does not prove. Fighter revenue depends on GE's F404 deliveries, which HAL does not control. The Su-30 engine order is domestic and contracted, so its risk is timing rather than demand. The AMCA and high-thrust programmes are years from revenue and carry technology risk, as the Kaveri result shows.

My view: the two capacity announcements are modest in cash terms, ₹218 crore and ₹335 crore, but they remove a real bottleneck on helicopters that HAL has already sold, and they speed up a contracted engine order. The Tejas Mk1A engine supply is the item that decides whether this year's numbers land.

Watch the next quarterly disclosure for Tejas Mk1A deliveries and any Air Force acceptance, the pace of AL-31FP deliveries against the 240-engine order, and any Cabinet Committee on Security decision on the Safran AMCA engine proposal.
