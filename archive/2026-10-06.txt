ETSA 2026: Consumer brands poised for bigger exits as strategic acquisitions pick up: Sauce’s Manu Chandra

Manu Chandra, founder, Sauce

India’s new-age consumer brands

are entering a phase offering larger exits to investors , with strategic acquisitions expected to become more frequent as companies scale and take market share from established players, according to Manu Chandra, founder and managing partner of consumer-focused VC firm Sauce.

Driving the news: “Five years from now, we will see many more smaller niche brands getting developed and many more frequent midsize acquisitions happening,” Chandra told ET in an interview.

Chandra won the

Midas Touch honour for best investor at The ET Startup Awards 2026, with the jury recognising his track record of backing companies early and delivering significant exits.

Innovist exit: In June, Sauce recorded one of its biggest exits yet, pocketing an estimated Rs 500-550 crore from L’Oréal’s acquisition of Innovist, in which it had invested Rs 50–55 crore. Chandra, a former investor at British PE firm 3i Group, founded Sauce in 2019 with a modest Rs 60 crore fund.

“If you think about M&A, many a time it makes a lot of sense for a company to acquire the synergistic or complementary strengths of the acquirer and pass on their strengths to them,” Chandra said.

Raising the stakes: The bar for an attractive consumer business has risen sharply. A company hitting Rs 100 crore revenue “does not excite us anymore,” Chandra said. He expects leading brands to scale considerably, with companies generating Rs 1,500-2,000 crore in revenue potentially delivering valuations of Rs 10,000-12,000 crore.

Physical AI startups moot industry body to set data-collection standards

Startups involved in collecting real-world data to train robots and other physical AI systems

are in early discussions to form an industry body to set common standards for workers’ pay and wellbeing, working hours, safety and legal compliance, founders told ET.

Tell me more: The proposed body could also help AI labs connect with various farms, factories, hotels and other real-world environments from where they can access and collect data needed to train robots at a fixed rate, one of the founders said.

Zoom out:

Physical AI data is currently recorded across three layers – residential, commercial and industrial.

Startups in the space, such as Human Archive, Humyn Labs, Neo Cambrian and Modal Robotics and Aura ML, are collecting data from created environments, factories, warehouses, cloud kitchens, hotels and homes to train robots and physical AI systems.

Industry insight: The global physical AI market is projected to reach $15.2 billion by 2032 from $1.5 billion in 2026, according to market research firm MarketsandMarkets, providing a huge opportunity for Indian startups to build across the value-chain.

Quick recap: In May, ET had reported that the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) took note of the developments around data recording, after Pronto’s pilots involving in-home data recording went viral.

UPI MDR spurs soundbox expansion beyond metros

Payment firms

are stepping up soundbox deployments and sales hiring in smaller towns, betting on UPI merchant discount rate (MDR) income to fund expansion.

Expansion plans: BharatPe plans about 50% more soundbox deployments than originally envisaged in selected markets. PhonePe plans over five million payment devices and 20,000-plus sales hires over 12 months. Pine Labs plans one million soundboxes.

How it works: The MDR framework takes effect October 15 . Fees from larger merchants could fund expansion among smaller shops that remain exempt. A proposed fund will also draw 5% of eligible MDR income to support merchant onboarding and payment infrastructure.

The opportunity: An NPCI official estimates the fund could reach Rs 3,000 crore over five years, with scope for soundbox deployments to grow from about 20 million to 50 million over time.

- SEC Clears 3x Leveraged Bitcoin and Ethereum Funds for Trading
  05 Oct 2026 | https://decrypt.co/380108/sec-clears-3x-leveraged-bitcoin-ethereum-funds
  The SEC approved a Cboe rule change on October 2 allowing six funds from Volatility Shares to list on a U.S. exchange, each targeting three times the daily price move of Bitcoin, Ethereum, gold, silver, crude oil, or natural gas. If Bitcoin futures rise 2% in a day, the fund aims to gain 6%; if they fall 2%, it aims to lose 6%. The funds get that exposure mainly through futures, contracts to buy or sell an asset at a set price later. ¶ The word "daily" matters. These funds reset every day, so the 3x promise applies one day at a time. Over longer stretches, returns drift far from three times the asset's move. If Bitcoin futures drop 10% Monday and rise 10% Tuesday, the asset ends down 1% while the fund ends down 9%. The SEC and FINRA have warned investors about exactly this. ¶ The approval follows SEC pushback on leverage: warning letters to nine issuers in December 2025 over products above 2x, and a March 2026 request to avoid 5x. The order sets no launch date; shares cannot trade until each fund's registration takes effect. Watch whether these funds attract real money or stay a niche trading tool, because daily-reset leverage punishes anyone who holds too long.

- HAL closes Tejas Mk1 order, adds Shakti engine line, buys out CAE training venture
  05 Oct 2026 | https://defence.in/threads/hal-equips-final-foc-tejas-trainers-with-israeli-el-m-2052-aesa-radars-as-iaf-prepares-for-mk1a-and-uttam-transition.18830/post-87015
  Hindustan Aeronautics Ltd packed three separate developments into the first week of October 2026: the last two Tejas Mk1 trainers left the legacy production line with new radars, a second Shakti helicopter engine line was announced at Koraput, and HAL agreed to buy CAE Canada's half of a helicopter training joint venture. ¶ The Tejas news concerns SPT7 and SPT8, the final two twin-seat trainers from the original 40-aircraft Mk1 order placed in December 2010. That contract split into 20 Initial Operational Clearance (IOC) aircraft and 20 Final Operational Clearance (FOC) aircraft, of which four were twin-seat trainers. HAL fitted these last two with the Israeli EL/M-2052 AESA radar and air-to-air refuelling probes. AESA stands for active electronically scanned array, a radar that steers its beam electronically rather than by moving a dish. Earlier Tejas trainers carried the older mechanically steered EL/M-2032. The point of the upgrade is pilot transition: the Indian Air Force wants trainees to meet the sensor setup they will use in the operational Tejas Mk1A combat variant. With these two airframes done, HAL says it is closing the 2010 order and moving its Tejas effort entirely to the Mk1A line. ¶ The Mk1A pipeline is where the volume sits. In 2021 the Ministry of Defence signed for 83 aircraft, 73 single-seat fighters and 10 twin-seat trainers. A follow-on order in 2025 added 97 more, including 29 twin-seat trainers. Those 29 are to carry the domestically developed Uttam AESA radar and the Swayam Raksha Kavach electronic warfare suite as standard, replacing the imported Israeli equipment used on the current FOC trainers. The article describes Uttam as having matured into production integration, though it gives no delivery schedule for the 97-aircraft batch. ¶ At Koraput in Odisha, Defence Minister Rajnath Singh is due to lay the foundation stone on Tuesday for a second Shakti engine production line, at an investment of ₹218 crore. The same event marks delivery of HAL Koraput's 2,000th aero-engine. Shakti is an Indo-French programme between HAL and Safran Helicopter Engines; the new line will build the Indian version of the Ardiden 1H1 turboshaft engine. A turboshaft is a gas turbine that drives a helicopter rotor. The stated demand driver is helicopter orders: the MoD has signed ₹62,700 crore of contracts with HAL for 156 Prachand light combat helicopters, 66 for the Air Force and 90 for the Army. Deliveries are to begin in the third year and run over five years. The government targets more than 65 per cent indigenous content in that contract, with more than 250 domestic suppliers, mostly small and medium enterprises. The Prachand is designed for operations above 5,000 metres. ¶ Koraput's history matters for judging the new line. The division opened in April 1964 building R11-F2/F2S turbojets for the MiG-21FL under licence, then R25 and R29B engines for the MiG-21BIS and MiG-27M, and from 2007-08 the RD-33 Series 3 for the MiG-29. A second facility there builds the AL-31FP for the Su-30MKI, under a 2004 Russian licence. In September 2024 the ministry signed a ₹26,000 crore contract for 240 AL-31FP engines, to be built at Koraput at 30 a year over eight years, with indigenous content rising to 63 per cent by the end of deliveries and averaging above 54 per cent. The division says it has built 2,000 engines and overhauled more than 7,700 over five decades. Separately, HAL and Safran signed a final contract in August 2026 through their joint venture SAFHAL Helicopter Engines for design, development, manufacture, supply and lifecycle support of the Aravalli, a 3,500-4,000 shaft horsepower turboshaft. ¶ On the training side, HAL has agreed to acquire CAE Canada's 50 per cent stake in HATSOFF Helicopter Training, comprising 38,404,205 equity shares of ₹10 face value each, at nil consideration. HATSOFF runs military and civil helicopter pilot training on simulators and is currently a 50:50 joint venture. After transfer it becomes a wholly owned HAL subsidiary. HAL expects the deal to close within about 60 days of the share transfer agreement. HAL shares traded at ₹4,648.90 on the BSE, up 0.74 per cent, giving a market capitalisation of ₹310,712.87 crore. The stock's 52-week high is ₹5,149.90, set on 17 August 2026, and its low ₹3,479.20 on 30 March 2026. Promoters hold 71.64 per cent. ¶ What is not established: the cost, capacity, construction timeline and commissioning date of the second Shakti line. Source 6 says the report it draws on does not provide those details, and Source 2 gives only the ₹218 crore investment figure. No delivery schedule is given for the 97-aircraft Mk1A order, and no first-flight or handover date appears for SPT7 and SPT8. The nil-consideration transfer raises an obvious question about why CAE is exiting for nothing, which none of the articles answers. ¶ The risks run along familiar lines. Engine work under licence is not the same as designing an engine from scratch. N K Samal, a retired Indian Air Force official, is quoted saying India still lacks expertise in high-temperature materials, turbine technology, combustion systems and engine controls, plus testing and certification, and that the HAL-Safran Aravalli programme is expected to bridge licensed manufacturing and genuine joint development. The Tejas programme carries its own transition risk: the trainers now flying carry imported radars, while the next 29 trainers must carry the indigenous Uttam and Swayam Raksha Kavach. Any slip in those systems lands on the Mk1A schedule. The Prachand order is large but back-loaded, with deliveries starting in year three, so the revenue arrives well after the ₹218 crore is spent. ¶ My view: the Tejas milestone is mostly symbolic, the end of a 2010 order, while the Shakti line and the Prachand contract are the parts that touch future revenue. The buyout of CAE is small in money terms but removes a partner from a training business HAL evidently wants to control. ¶ Watch the foundation event for the Shakti line's capacity and commissioning date, any official word on Uttam radar deliveries for the 29 new trainers, and confirmation that the HATSOFF share transfer completes inside the stated 60 days.
