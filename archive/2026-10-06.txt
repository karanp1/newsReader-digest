ETSA 2026: Consumer brands poised for bigger exits as strategic acquisitions pick up: Sauce’s Manu Chandra

Manu Chandra, founder, Sauce

India’s new-age consumer brands

are entering a phase offering larger exits to investors , with strategic acquisitions expected to become more frequent as companies scale and take market share from established players, according to Manu Chandra, founder and managing partner of consumer-focused VC firm Sauce.

Driving the news: “Five years from now, we will see many more smaller niche brands getting developed and many more frequent midsize acquisitions happening,” Chandra told ET in an interview.

Chandra won the

Midas Touch honour for best investor at The ET Startup Awards 2026, with the jury recognising his track record of backing companies early and delivering significant exits.

Innovist exit: In June, Sauce recorded one of its biggest exits yet, pocketing an estimated Rs 500-550 crore from L’Oréal’s acquisition of Innovist, in which it had invested Rs 50–55 crore. Chandra, a former investor at British PE firm 3i Group, founded Sauce in 2019 with a modest Rs 60 crore fund.

“If you think about M&A, many a time it makes a lot of sense for a company to acquire the synergistic or complementary strengths of the acquirer and pass on their strengths to them,” Chandra said.

Raising the stakes: The bar for an attractive consumer business has risen sharply. A company hitting Rs 100 crore revenue “does not excite us anymore,” Chandra said. He expects leading brands to scale considerably, with companies generating Rs 1,500-2,000 crore in revenue potentially delivering valuations of Rs 10,000-12,000 crore.

Physical AI startups moot industry body to set data-collection standards

Startups involved in collecting real-world data to train robots and other physical AI systems

are in early discussions to form an industry body to set common standards for workers’ pay and wellbeing, working hours, safety and legal compliance, founders told ET.

Tell me more: The proposed body could also help AI labs connect with various farms, factories, hotels and other real-world environments from where they can access and collect data needed to train robots at a fixed rate, one of the founders said.

Zoom out:

Physical AI data is currently recorded across three layers – residential, commercial and industrial.

Startups in the space, such as Human Archive, Humyn Labs, Neo Cambrian and Modal Robotics and Aura ML, are collecting data from created environments, factories, warehouses, cloud kitchens, hotels and homes to train robots and physical AI systems.

Industry insight: The global physical AI market is projected to reach $15.2 billion by 2032 from $1.5 billion in 2026, according to market research firm MarketsandMarkets, providing a huge opportunity for Indian startups to build across the value-chain.

Quick recap: In May, ET had reported that the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) took note of the developments around data recording, after Pronto’s pilots involving in-home data recording went viral.

UPI MDR spurs soundbox expansion beyond metros

Payment firms

are stepping up soundbox deployments and sales hiring in smaller towns, betting on UPI merchant discount rate (MDR) income to fund expansion.

Expansion plans: BharatPe plans about 50% more soundbox deployments than originally envisaged in selected markets. PhonePe plans over five million payment devices and 20,000-plus sales hires over 12 months. Pine Labs plans one million soundboxes.

How it works: The MDR framework takes effect October 15 . Fees from larger merchants could fund expansion among smaller shops that remain exempt. A proposed fund will also draw 5% of eligible MDR income to support merchant onboarding and payment infrastructure.

The opportunity: An NPCI official estimates the fund could reach Rs 3,000 crore over five years, with scope for soundbox deployments to grow from about 20 million to 50 million over time.

  A new study in PNAS Nexus finds that Werner Heisenberg's Nazi nuclear reactor was nowhere near critical, contradicting his 1947 claim that it needed only a little more uranium. The B8 pile, built in a Haigerloch church cellar, had a neutron multiplication factor of 0.942; a reactor needs 1.0. Its uranium cubes were too porous and too close together, and its heavy water was only 96.8% pure. Even all German facilities combined held just 2,572 kg of uranium, short of the 2,994 kg needed. - SEC Clears 3x Leveraged Bitcoin and Ethereum Funds for Trading
  05 Oct 2026 | https://decrypt.co/380108/sec-clears-3x-leveraged-bitcoin-ethereum-funds
  The SEC approved a Cboe rule change on October 2 allowing six funds from Volatility Shares to list on a U.S. exchange, each targeting three times the daily price move of Bitcoin, Ethereum, gold, silver, crude oil, or natural gas. If Bitcoin futures rise 2% in a day, the fund aims to gain 6%; if they fall 2%, it aims to lose 6%. The funds get that exposure mainly through futures, contracts to buy or sell an asset at a set price later. ¶ The word "daily" matters. These funds reset every day, so the 3x promise applies one day at a time. Over longer stretches, returns drift far from three times the asset's move. If Bitcoin futures drop 10% Monday and rise 10% Tuesday, the asset ends down 1% while the fund ends down 9%. The SEC and FINRA have warned investors about exactly this. ¶ The approval follows SEC pushback on leverage: warning letters to nine issuers in December 2025 over products above 2x, and a March 2026 request to avoid 5x. The order sets no launch date; shares cannot trade until each fund's registration takes effect. Watch whether these funds attract real money or stay a niche trading tool, because daily-reset leverage punishes anyone who holds too long.

- HAL closes Tejas Mk1 order, adds Shakti engine line, buys out CAE training venture
  05 Oct 2026 | https://defence.in/threads/hal-equips-final-foc-tejas-trainers-with-israeli-el-m-2052-aesa-radars-as-iaf-prepares-for-mk1a-and-uttam-transition.18830/post-87015
  HAL had three pieces of news on 5 October 2026: the last two Tejas Mk1 trainers left the legacy order with new radars fitted, a second Shakti engine line was announced at Koraput, and HAL agreed to buy CAE's half of a helicopter training joint venture. ¶ Start with the Tejas trainers. The final two twin-seat trainers from the original 40-aircraft Tejas Mk1 order, placed in December 2010, have been fitted with Israeli EL/M-2052 AESA radars and air-to-air refuelling probes. An AESA radar is a flat-panel radar that steers its beam electronically instead of with a moving dish. The two aircraft are designated SPT7 and SPT8. Earlier Tejas trainers carried the older mechanically steered EL/M-2032 radar. The 2010 order split into 20 aircraft at Initial Operational Clearance standard and 20 at Final Operational Clearance standard, and the FOC half included four twin-seat trainers. With SPT7 and SPT8 fitted, that order is finished and HAL says it will now focus entirely on the Mk1A production lines. The trainers are meant to give pilots hands-on time with the sensor setup they will meet in the combat version of the Mk1A. ¶ The Mk1A pipeline is where the volume sits. In 2021 the Ministry of Defence signed for 83 Tejas Mk1A aircraft, 73 single-seat fighters and 10 twin-seat trainers. A follow-on order in 2025 added 97 more Mk1A jets, including 29 twin-seat trainers. Those 29 trainers are required to carry the domestically developed Uttam AESA radar and the Swayam Raksha Kavach electronic warfare suite as standard equipment. Electronic warfare suites detect, jam or spoof enemy radar and missile seekers. So the current FOC trainers fly with imported Israeli avionics, while the next batch is specified with Indian radar and Indian electronic warfare gear. Source 1 describes the Uttam radar as having matured into production integration, which is the claim that matters for the import substitution story. ¶ Second, engines. HAL will build a second production line for the Shakti engine at its Koraput facility in Odisha, at an investment of ₹218 crore. Defence Minister Rajnath Singh is due to lay the foundation stone on Tuesday, 6 October, at the Sunabeda site in Koraput district, and to attend an event marking delivery of HAL Koraput's 2,000th aero-engine. The ministry says the extra capacity is for indigenous helicopter platforms, naming the Advanced Light Helicopter Dhruv and the Light Combat Helicopter Prachand. The demand case is the ₹62,700 crore contract with HAL for 156 Prachand helicopters, 66 for the Air Force and 90 for the Army, with deliveries starting in the third year and running over five years. The government aims for more than 65 per cent indigenous content during that contract, with more than 250 domestic companies, mostly small and medium enterprises, involved. The LCH is built for high-altitude operations above 5,000 metres. ¶ The Shakti is an Indo-French programme between HAL and Safran Helicopter Engines; the second line will build the Indian version of the Ardiden 1H1 turboshaft engine. Koraput's history runs from licensed R11-F2/F2S turbojets for the MiG-21FL from April 1964, through R25 and R29B engines for the MiG-21BIS and MiG-27M, RD-33 Series 3 engines for the MiG-29 from 2007-08, and AL-31FP engines for the Su-30MKI from 2004 under Russian licence. The division says it has made 2,000 engines and overhauled more than 7,700 over five decades. In September 2024 the ministry signed a ₹26,000 crore contract for 240 AL-31FP engines, to be built at Koraput at 30 a year over eight years, with indigenous content rising to 63 per cent by the end of deliveries and averaging above 54 per cent. In August this year HAL and Safran signed a final contract through their joint venture SAFHAL Helicopter Engines for design, development, manufacture, supply and lifecycle support of the Aravalli, a 3,500-4,000 shaft horsepower turboshaft. Shaft horsepower is a measure of the power delivered to a helicopter's rotor. ¶ Third, training. HAL has agreed to acquire CAE Canada's 50 per cent stake in HATSOFF Helicopter Training, comprising 38,404,205 equity shares of ₹10 face value each, at nil consideration. HATSOFF is a 50:50 joint venture that runs simulators for military and civil helicopter pilot training. After the transfer, HATSOFF becomes a wholly owned HAL subsidiary. HAL expects the deal to close within about 60 days of signing the share transfer agreement. The company says HATSOFF's line of business matches its own. On the same day HAL shares traded at ₹4,648.90 on the BSE, up 0.74 per cent from the previous close of ₹4,614.95, with a market capitalisation of ₹310,712.87 crore. The stock's 52-week high was ₹5,149.90 on 17 August 2026 and its low ₹3,479.20 on 30 March 2026. Promoters held 71.64 per cent, institutions 21.32 per cent and non-institutions 7.04 per cent. Those market figures come from a financial news report, not from HAL. ¶ What is not confirmed matters here. Source 5, reporting on the Sunabeda ceremony, says the cost, capacity, construction timeline and commissioning schedule of the new facilities have not been disclosed, and that those details will determine the scale and immediate operational impact. That sits awkwardly beside the ₹218 crore figure carried by Sources 2, 4 and 6, so treat the capacity and timing as open. HAL has not said when the first Mk1A trainer with the Uttam radar will fly, and no source gives a delivery schedule for the 97-aircraft order. The nil-consideration transfer of the CAE stake has no stated value, so the accounting effect is unclear. Sources also differ on the venue: Source 2 says the second Shakti line comes up at the Koraput facility, while Source 5 places the foundation ceremony at Sunabeda in Koraput district. Sunabeda is where the Koraput division's engine work sits, so these are likely the same site described two ways, but the articles do not say so. ¶ The risks are the usual ones for a state-linked defence builder. Engine work is the hardest part of the chain: Source 2 quotes retired Air Force official N K Samal saying India still lacks expertise in high-temperature materials, turbine technology, combustion systems, engine controls, and testing and certification, and that licensed manufacture with rising local content is a different skill from designing an engine from scratch. The Aravalli joint development is described as a bridge toward that capability, not proof of it. The Tejas programme still depends on Israeli radar for the aircraft now being delivered, and the shift to Uttam and the SRK suite is a specification for future aircraft, not a demonstrated one. Prachand revenue is back-loaded, with deliveries starting only in the third year of the contract. The HATSOFF purchase removes a partner from a training business whose returns are not disclosed. ¶ My view: the Tejas milestone is mostly symbolic, since it closes a 2010 order rather than adding revenue, while the Shakti line and the 156-helicopter Prachand contract are the items that change HAL's engine volumes and recurring aftermarket work. The HATSOFF buyout is small and administrative, tidying a joint venture into a subsidiary. ¶ Watch the foundation ceremony on 6 October for the capacity figure and commissioning date of the second Shakti line, any statement on Uttam radar production rates, and the closing of the HATSOFF share transfer within the stated 60 days.

- The eternal complement
  06 Oct 2026 | https://openai.com/index/the-eternal-complement/
  The essay argues that human progress has always depended less on raw genius than on the machinery and bureaucracy needed to turn ideas into reality, and that AI is about to change which of those two things is scarce. The authors point to the James Webb Space Telescope: Galileo widened our view of the universe with two lenses and a tube, while Webb required eighteen mirror segments engineered to fifty nanometer precision, three hundred organizations across fourteen countries, and ten billion dollars. That gap is the point. Research productivity has been falling even as spending rises. Sustaining Moore's law now takes more than eighteen times as many researchers as it did in the early 1970s, and economy-wide research productivity fell by a factor of forty-one while research effort rose twenty-three-fold. The technician workforce is growing twice as fast as the scientists. ¶ The authors call the unglamorous capacity to execute "institutional intelligence," and treat it as a complement to frontier intelligence: more of one raises the value of the other. Their bet is that AI will spend most of its time on the execution side, writing code, searching literature, turning sketches into prototypes. That would let one person pursue projects that once needed a whole organization, and push creative work outside the institutions that currently gatekeep it. The thing to watch is whether that produces a wave of independent work or just a new set of bottlenecks. My view: the essay is right that execution, not ideas, is the binding constraint, and that is exactly why the incumbents with the biggest bureaucracies stand to gain most.
