ETSA 2026: Consumer brands poised for bigger exits as strategic acquisitions pick up: Sauce’s Manu Chandra

Manu Chandra, founder, Sauce

India’s new-age consumer brands

are entering a phase offering larger exits to investors , with strategic acquisitions expected to become more frequent as companies scale and take market share from established players, according to Manu Chandra, founder and managing partner of consumer-focused VC firm Sauce.

Driving the news: “Five years from now, we will see many more smaller niche brands getting developed and many more frequent midsize acquisitions happening,” Chandra told ET in an interview.

Chandra won the

Midas Touch honour for best investor at The ET Startup Awards 2026, with the jury recognising his track record of backing companies early and delivering significant exits.

Innovist exit: In June, Sauce recorded one of its biggest exits yet, pocketing an estimated Rs 500-550 crore from L’Oréal’s acquisition of Innovist, in which it had invested Rs 50–55 crore. Chandra, a former investor at British PE firm 3i Group, founded Sauce in 2019 with a modest Rs 60 crore fund.

“If you think about M&A, many a time it makes a lot of sense for a company to acquire the synergistic or complementary strengths of the acquirer and pass on their strengths to them,” Chandra said.

Raising the stakes: The bar for an attractive consumer business has risen sharply. A company hitting Rs 100 crore revenue “does not excite us anymore,” Chandra said. He expects leading brands to scale considerably, with companies generating Rs 1,500-2,000 crore in revenue potentially delivering valuations of Rs 10,000-12,000 crore.

Physical AI startups moot industry body to set data-collection standards

Startups involved in collecting real-world data to train robots and other physical AI systems

are in early discussions to form an industry body to set common standards for workers’ pay and wellbeing, working hours, safety and legal compliance, founders told ET.

Tell me more: The proposed body could also help AI labs connect with various farms, factories, hotels and other real-world environments from where they can access and collect data needed to train robots at a fixed rate, one of the founders said.

Zoom out:

Physical AI data is currently recorded across three layers – residential, commercial and industrial.

Startups in the space, such as Human Archive, Humyn Labs, Neo Cambrian and Modal Robotics and Aura ML, are collecting data from created environments, factories, warehouses, cloud kitchens, hotels and homes to train robots and physical AI systems.

Industry insight: The global physical AI market is projected to reach $15.2 billion by 2032 from $1.5 billion in 2026, according to market research firm MarketsandMarkets, providing a huge opportunity for Indian startups to build across the value-chain.

Quick recap: In May, ET had reported that the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) took note of the developments around data recording, after Pronto’s pilots involving in-home data recording went viral.

UPI MDR spurs soundbox expansion beyond metros

Payment firms

are stepping up soundbox deployments and sales hiring in smaller towns, betting on UPI merchant discount rate (MDR) income to fund expansion.

Expansion plans: BharatPe plans about 50% more soundbox deployments than originally envisaged in selected markets. PhonePe plans over five million payment devices and 20,000-plus sales hires over 12 months. Pine Labs plans one million soundboxes.

How it works: The MDR framework takes effect October 15 . Fees from larger merchants could fund expansion among smaller shops that remain exempt. A proposed fund will also draw 5% of eligible MDR income to support merchant onboarding and payment infrastructure.

The opportunity: An NPCI official estimates the fund could reach Rs 3,000 crore over five years, with scope for soundbox deployments to grow from about 20 million to 50 million over time.

- SEC Clears 3x Leveraged Bitcoin and Ethereum Funds for Trading
  05 Oct 2026 | https://decrypt.co/380108/sec-clears-3x-leveraged-bitcoin-ethereum-funds
  The SEC approved a Cboe rule change on October 2 allowing six funds from Volatility Shares to list on a U.S. exchange, each targeting three times the daily price move of Bitcoin, Ethereum, gold, silver, crude oil, or natural gas. If Bitcoin futures rise 2% in a day, the fund aims to gain 6%; if they fall 2%, it aims to lose 6%. The funds get that exposure mainly through futures, contracts to buy or sell an asset at a set price later. ¶ The word "daily" matters. These funds reset every day, so the 3x promise applies one day at a time. Over longer stretches, returns drift far from three times the asset's move. If Bitcoin futures drop 10% Monday and rise 10% Tuesday, the asset ends down 1% while the fund ends down 9%. The SEC and FINRA have warned investors about exactly this. ¶ The approval follows SEC pushback on leverage: warning letters to nine issuers in December 2025 over products above 2x, and a March 2026 request to avoid 5x. The order sets no launch date; shares cannot trade until each fund's registration takes effect. Watch whether these funds attract real money or stay a niche trading tool, because daily-reset leverage punishes anyone who holds too long.

  05 Oct 2026 | https://defence.in/threads/hal-equips-final-foc-tejas-trainers-with-israeli-el-m-2052-aesa-radars-as-iaf-prepares-for-mk1a-and-uttam-transition.18830/post-87015
  Hindustan Aeronautics Limited packed three separate developments into the first week of October 2026, and they point in the same direction: finishing old orders, adding capacity for new ones, and pulling a training joint venture fully in-house. ¶ The most concrete number is ₹218 crore. That is the investment HAL is making in a second production line for the Shakti engine at its Koraput facility in Odisha. Defence Minister Rajnath Singh is scheduled to lay the foundation stone on Tuesday, 6 October 2026, according to the Ministry of Defence. The same event marks the delivery of Koraput's 2,000th aero-engine. Odisha Chief Minister Mohan Charan Majhi, Defence Production Secretary Sanjeev Kumar and HAL Chairman and Managing Director Ravi K are expected to attend. ¶ The Shakti is a turboshaft engine, meaning it powers helicopters rather than fixed-wing jets. It is built through an Indo-French joint programme between HAL and Safran Helicopter Engines, and the new line will manufacture the Indian version of the Ardiden 1H1. The customers are the Dhruv Advanced Light Helicopter and the Prachand Light Combat Helicopter. Demand for those platforms just got much larger: the Ministry of Defence has signed contracts with HAL worth ₹62,700 crore for 156 Prachand helicopters, split as 66 for the Indian Air Force and 90 for the Army. Deliveries begin in the third year and run over five years. The government says it wants more than 65 per cent indigenous content during that contract, with more than 250 domestic companies, mostly small and medium enterprises, involved. The Prachand is designed to operate above 5,000 metres, which is why engine supply matters: thin air at altitude demands reliable power. ¶ Koraput's history explains why the milestone is being treated as an event. The division opened in April 1964 building R11-F2/F2S turbojets for the MiG-21FL under licence, then moved through R25 and R29 engines for later MiG variants, RD-33 Series 3 engines for the MiG-29 from 2007-08, and AL-31FP engines for the Su-30MKI from 2004 under Russian licence. It has now built 2,000 engines and overhauled more than 7,700 aero-engines over five decades. In September 2024 the Defence Ministry signed a ₹26,000 crore contract for 240 AL-31FP engines, to be made at Koraput at 30 a year over eight years, with indigenous content rising to 63 per cent by the end of the programme and averaging above 54 per cent. In August this year HAL and Safran signed a final contract through their joint venture SAFHAL Helicopter Engines for the design, development, manufacture, supply and lifecycle support of the Aravalli, a 3,500 to 4,000 shaft horsepower turboshaft. ¶ On the fighter side, HAL has fitted the last two twin-seat Tejas trainers from the original 2010 order with Israeli EL/M-2052 AESA radars and air-to-air refuelling probes. AESA stands for active electronically scanned array, a radar that steers its beam electronically instead of mechanically. The two aircraft, designated SPT7 and SPT8, close out the 40-jet Tejas Mk1 order placed in December 2010, which was split into 20 Initial Operational Clearance and 20 Final Operational Clearance aircraft, including four twin-seat trainers. Earlier trainers carried the older mechanically steered EL/M-2032 radar. The point of the upgrade is pilot transition: trainees will meet the sensor setup they will use in the Mk1A combat variant. ¶ The Mk1A pipeline is where the volume sits. In 2021 the Ministry of Defence signed for 83 Tejas Mk1A aircraft, 73 single-seat fighters and 10 twin-seat trainers. In 2025 a follow-on order for 97 more Mk1A jets added 29 twin-seat trainers. Those 29 trainers are mandated to carry the domestically developed Uttam AESA radar and the Swayam Raksha Kavach electronic warfare suite as baseline equipment, replacing the imported Israeli avionics used on the aircraft just delivered. That is a genuine shift in the supply chain, not just a label. ¶ The third item is small but tidy. HAL has agreed to acquire CAE Canada's 50 per cent stake in HATSOFF Helicopter Training, comprising 38,404,205 equity shares of ₹10 face value each, at nil consideration. HATSOFF runs military and civil helicopter pilot training on simulators. Once the shares transfer, expected within about 60 days of the share transfer agreement, HATSOFF becomes a wholly owned HAL subsidiary instead of a 50:50 joint venture. HAL's filing says the line of business aligns with its main business. ¶ What is not yet known is how much of the new Shakti line's output is already contracted, and whether the ₹218 crore covers the full build-out or just the first phase. The articles give no delivery schedule for the second line, no capacity figure in engines per year, and no breakdown of how much of the Prachand's ₹62,700 crore value flows to HAL's engine division versus the airframe side. The nil-consideration transfer of the CAE stake also raises a question the filings do not answer: why CAE exited for nothing, and whether HAL is absorbing liabilities along with the shares. ¶ The risks are the familiar ones for this business. Engine manufacturing under licence is not the same as designing an engine from scratch, and the articles quote N K Samal, a retired Indian Air Force official, saying India still lacks expertise in high-temperature materials, turbine technology, combustion systems, engine controls, and testing and certification. The Aravalli joint development with Safran is described as a bridge between licensed manufacturing and genuine joint development, which is a polite way of saying the hardest part is still ahead. On the Tejas side, the shift from Israeli to Indian radar and electronic warfare kit puts execution risk on domestic suppliers; any delay there feeds directly into the 97-aircraft order. And the Prachand contract's indigenous content target of over 65 per cent depends on hundreds of smaller suppliers delivering. ¶ What to watch next: whether the second Shakti line gets a stated annual capacity and delivery schedule, whether the Uttam radar and Swayam Raksha Kavach suite stay on schedule for the 29 new trainers, and how quickly the HATSOFF share transfer closes within its 60-day window. The stock traded at ₹4,648.90 on the BSE on 5 October 2026, up 0.74 per cent, with a market capitalisation of ₹310,712.87 crore and promoter holding of 71.64 per cent, according to Accord Fintech. ¶ My view: the Shakti line is the most material item here because it adds physical capacity against a ₹62,700 crore helicopter order that already exists, while the Tejas trainer upgrade is a tidy close-out of a 2010 contract rather than new revenue. The HATSOFF buyout is housekeeping, but it does put simulator training revenue and capability fully inside HAL rather than shared with a partner.

  06 Oct 2026 | https://openai.com/index/the-eternal-complement/
  The essay argues that human progress has always depended less on raw genius than on the machinery and bureaucracy needed to turn ideas into reality, and that AI is about to change which of those two things is scarce. The authors point to the James Webb Space Telescope: Galileo widened our view of the universe with two lenses and a tube, while Webb required eighteen mirror segments engineered to fifty nanometer precision, three hundred organizations across fourteen countries, and ten billion dollars. That gap is the point. Research productivity has been falling even as spending rises. Sustaining Moore's law now takes more than eighteen times as many researchers as it did in the early 1970s, and economy-wide research productivity fell by a factor of forty-one while research effort rose twenty-three-fold. The technician workforce is growing twice as fast as the scientists. ¶ The authors call the unglamorous capacity to execute "institutional intelligence," and treat it as a complement to frontier intelligence: more of one raises the value of the other. Their bet is that AI will spend most of its time on the execution side, writing code, searching literature, turning sketches into prototypes. That would let one person pursue projects that once needed a whole organization, and push creative work outside the institutions that currently gatekeep it. The thing to watch is whether that produces a wave of independent work or just a new set of bottlenecks. My view: the essay is right that execution, not ideas, is the binding constraint, and that is exactly why the incumbents with the biggest bureaucracies stand to gain most.
