No need to slow AI deployment if technology clears safety test: ElevenLabs CEO

Mati Staniszewski, cofounder and CEO, ElevenLabs. (Photo by N Narasimha Murthy)

Voice AI pioneer ElevenLabs

does not support an industry-wide slowdown in AI development, cofounder and CEO Mati Staniszewski told ET in an interview. He said companies should slow deployment where technology is found to be “unsafe,” but move ahead where it has been “adequately tested.”

Quote, unquote: “There is increasing collaboration across the industry in the space to make sure that people work together to assess this risk,” he said when asked about the increasing calls to pace AI. “But we need to balance the benefits for society and the risks,” the 31-year-old Staniszewski said on the sidelines of ElevenLabs’ Bengaluru event on October 6.

His comments come amid growing calls to pace AI development, including from Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei.

India market: The London-headquartered firm completed a $300 million employee tender offer last week, doubling its valuation to $22 billion in roughly eight months. India remains its second-biggest market and is growing rapidly.

“I think... it’s a voice-first country… When we started the company, the highest usage, the highest pickup, the highest set of new use cases came from India,” Staniszewski said.

Expanding market: He also said ElevenLabs plans to expand its AI voice technology to all 22 officially recognised Indian languages in the coming months. The company currently supports 14 Indian languages on its latest text-to-speech (TTS) model, Eleven V4, which was launched last week.

Beyond voice: The company is pushing its Eleven Agents platform for enterprise use cases. The platform allows businesses to create, deploy, and monitor conversational AI agents across more than 90 languages, with enterprise security and controls.

Prosus steps up India fintech bets with Kiwi, Oolka funding talks

Amsterdam-listed technology investor Prosus

is in talks to invest in fintech startups Kiwi and Oolka, stepping up direct bets on Indian financial services companies.

Driving the news: Kiwi is currently looking to raise around $40 million, as ET reported last week . Prosus has since held exploratory discussions to invest in the credit-on-UPI startup, though the talks are at an early stage.

Separately, AI-led credit management startup Oolka is in advanced discussions with Prosus and Bessemer Venture Partners to raise $40-50 million at a $200-million post-money valuation.

The bigger picture: Prosus’ India fintech exposure had long been anchored by PayU, but it has widened direct investments across lending, housing finance and savings. It invested in Mintifi and Vastu Housing Finance in 2024, announced a $100-million investment in Navi this August, and is also in talks to back savings startup Bachatt.

Garuda Aerospace raises $10 million at $320 million valuation ahead of IPO

Agnishwar Jayaprakash, founder and CEO, Garuda Aerospace

Chennai-based drone technology company Garuda Aerospace

has raised about $10 million in a pre-IPO funding round at a $320 million pre-money valuation.

The round was led by the Motherson family office and Dubai-based Aditum Investment Group.

IPO details: Garuda is targeting a public listing in Q1 2027. The company filed the draft IPO papers through the confidential route in April and received Sebi’s observations on August 5, allowing it to proceed.

The proposed IPO is expected to be around Rs 1,000 crore, comprising a fresh issue of up to Rs 750 crore and an offer-for-sale component of around Rs 250 crore.

SBI Capital Markets, Axis Capital, ICICI Securities and IIFL Capital Services are expected to be among the book-running lead managers.

Garuda had earlier targeted a valuation of Rs 4,000–5,000 crore for the IPO.

About the company: Founded in 2015 by Agnishwar Jayaprakash, Garuda develops drones and provides drone-as-a-service solutions across agriculture, defence, infrastructure, surveillance and industrial applications.

Revenue from operations rose 67% to Rs 206 crore in FY26 from Rs 123 crore in FY25, while net profit increased 41% to Rs 26 crore from Rs 18 crore, per its FY26 annual report.

- Analysis: Why NASA wants to build a nuclear reactor on the moon
  07 Oct 2026 | https://www.thehindu.com/sci-tech/science/analysis-why-nasa-wants-to-build-nuclear-reactor-on-the-moon/article71552807.ece
  NASA plans a 20 kW nuclear reactor near the moon's south pole by 2030, enough for a few households on Earth. Russia and China's ILRS reactor would produce about 10 kW. Across 24 hours, liquidations reached about $1.02 billion. ¶ The mechanism is worth understanding. In crypto derivatives, a liquidation happens when a leveraged trader's collateral can no longer cover losses, so the exchange automatically closes the position by selling into the market. Those forced sales push prices down, which triggers more liquidations. Bitcoin absorbed about $209 million of the damage, Ethereum $87 million, Solana $27 million and XRP $11 million. ¶ The trigger was a technical breakdown below key on-chain support levels, the price zones where buyers had previously defended the market.   06 Oct 2026 | https://fortune.com/article/current-price-of-gold-10-06-2026/

The article notes prices have hit record highs, up more than 25% since early 2025, driven by persistent inflation and economic uncertainty. ¶ For a non-specialist, the useful distinction is between owning metal and owning exposure. Physical bars, coins and jewelry are one route, but most gold trading happens through exchange-traded funds, which track the gold price without anyone storing a bar. Financial advisor James Taska says rebalancing a client's gold allocation is much easier through an ETF, and that buy and sell spreads on physical gold can be wide. The trade-off is real: from 1971 to 2024, stocks averaged 10.7% annual returns against gold's 7.9%, so gold has cost long-term performance even as it hedges inflation. ¶ My view: treat gold as portfolio insurance sized to what you can afford to underperform, not as a growth bet. The Shakti engine is co-developed with France's Safran and powers the Dhruv Advanced Light Helicopter, its armed Rudra variant and the Prachand Light Combat Helicopter. HAL currently builds 80 Ardiden 1H1 Shakti engines a year at its Bengaluru facility, so the new Koraput line takes group capacity to 180 engines a year. Each Prachand carries two Shakti engines. ¶ The order book behind the helicopter line is concrete. The armed forces have ordered 156 Prachands under contracts worth ₹62,700 crore signed in March 2025, with 66 for the Air Force and 90 for the Army. ICICI Securities, a brokerage, lists HAL's existing helicopter backlog as 25 Dhruv ALH, 12 Light Utility Helicopters, 156 Prachand LCH and 10 Dhruv NG ordered by Pawan Hans. A further 110 LUH and 95 LCH are planned but contracts have not been placed. The brokerage also notes HAL plans roughly ₹12,000 crore of investment in manufacturing infrastructure by 2030 across the LCA, HTT-40 and LCH programmes. ¶ On the fighter side, the AL-31FP expansion is tied to a September 2024 Ministry of Defence contract worth over ₹26,000 crore for 240 engines for the Su-30MKI fleet. HAL was originally to execute that order over eight years. It has delivered 63 engines so far, against a target of 60 by the end of the second year, and the government says the first engine came ahead of schedule despite global supply-chain and raw-material problems. HAL estimates the Air Force will need around 900 engines over the Su-30 fleet's lifetime. The Air Force operates 260 Su-30s and will receive 12 more to replace aircraft lost in accidents. Engines are built at Koraput from the raw-material stage under technology transfer from Russia, with only some spares, forgings and castings imported. ¶ The Tejas Mk1A programme remains the open question. It is running behind schedule, partly because US firm GE Aerospace has been slow to supply F404-IN20 engines. HAL has received 10 engines, according to Jefferies, a brokerage. HAL's chairman and managing director, Ravi Kota, said in a media interview after completing the Tejas Mk1 trainer contract on September 18, 2026 that he expects to deliver Tejas Mk1A aircraft this year. Jefferies reads the 10 engines as supporting delivery of 10 aircraft this year, against its own assumption of five, and calls the Tejas Mk1A 43 per cent of HAL's order book. The brokerage has a Buy rating and a ₹6,800 target, and says the stock de-rated on Mk1A delays since calendar 2024. It adds that the Air Force accepting even one aircraft would lift confidence in HAL's execution. HAL is also negotiating with GE Aerospace on joint production of F414 engines in India for the LCA Mk2. ¶ Longer-dated propulsion plans were aired at the same event. Singh referred to a proposed investment of around ₹5,000 crore in an indigenous high-thrust aero-engine programme, including India's first indigenous high-thrust engine testing facility and indigenisation of raw materials with Mishra Dhatu Nigam. India is also in talks with Safran and Rolls Royce on domestic engine capability. The Tejas Mk1A delivery count for this year rests on the chairman's stated confidence and 10 engines received, not on a signed-off schedule. ¶ The risks are execution and dependence. The Tejas Mk1A engine supply is the item that decides whether this year's numbers land. ¶ Watch the next quarterly disclosure for Tejas Mk1A deliveries and any Air Force acceptance, the pace of AL-31FP deliveries against the 240-engine order, and any Cabinet Committee on Security decision on the Safran AMCA engine proposal. Consumer agents like ChatGPT can increasingly research and buy products, while Apparel Group is using AI for forecasting, replenishment, pricing and scheduling across 85 brands and 2,500 stores. The winners may be retailers whose data stays visible and usable to agents. Read more →
