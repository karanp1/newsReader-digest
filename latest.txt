### ET Startup Awards 2026: India's Startup Story Is Only Getting Started
12 Oct 2026 | https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/tech/newsletters/morning-dispatch/the-et-startup-awards-2026-special/articleshow/134880370.cms

Piyush Goyal looked at a room of 300 founders and investors and said the quiet part out loud: the best thing that could happen to India is that its best people stop leaving.

His argument ran against a decade of received wisdom. For 25 to 30 years, India's top engineers went to America on H-1B visas, and the results are visible on every street in Silicon Valley. Goyal's counterfactual is uncomfortable. What if they had built here? He called the H-1B curbs good for the Indian economy, not a wound to dress but a door closing on an old habit.

The timing made the point sharper. This was the 12th edition of the ET Startup Awards, held in Bengaluru as artificial intelligence and deep tech rise and homegrown companies tap public markets and chase global ambitions. About 250,000 startups are registered in India. Goyal noted that the real count that matters, the number of significant deep tech firms, is still small.

Groww took ET Startup of the Year 2026, joining a list that already holds Urban Company, Lenskart, Razorpay, Zomato, Zerodha, Delhivery, Swiggy, OfBusiness, Freshworks, Oyo and Ola. The honour is a lagging indicator. The companies on it were once experiments. Now they are institutions.

Sameer Nigam of PhonePe said the landscape had widened dramatically in a decade, with innovation spreading well beyond its early strongholds. Harshil Mathur of Razorpay, whose company won the same award in 2022, said the strength of this year's field showed how far Indian entrepreneurship has come.

On trade, Goyal was careful. He described India-US relations as intrinsically engaged but always carrying friction points, a relationship both sides recognise as consequential. On foreign investment outflows, he pointed to the greater attraction of Western technology stocks and the narrowing interest rate gap between US and Indian bonds. Some slowing of flows, he said, is natural.

My view: the awards matter less than the direction they signal. A country that once exported its founders is now being asked to keep them, and the keeping is the whole game.

### List When the Business Is Predictable, and Stay Transparent After: Groww, Meesho Founders
12 Oct 2026 | https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/tech/newsletters/morning-dispatch/the-et-startup-awards-2026-special/articleshow/134880370.cms

Vidit Aatrey and Lalit Keshre listed their companies a month apart late last year. On a panel at the ET Startup Awards 2026, moderated by ETtech editor Samidha Sharma, they disagreed about why founders are rushing to the public markets earlier than before.

Aatrey sees a practical shift. In the US, small IPOs are rare. In India, both small and large listings happen. Founders look at that and ask a direct question: can I short-circuit the private fundraising and go public instead? Public markets, in this reading, are not a graduation ceremony. They are an alternative route.

Keshre put the question differently. Capital, he said, is available where capital is needed. The problem is valuation. At a certain stage, valuing an asset is very tough. In private markets, two parties decide. In public markets, a lot more parties do. Groww listed in November, and Meesho followed, two decade-old companies choosing the same door for different reasons.

Both agreed on the precondition. Startups should go public only when their businesses have achieved a degree of predictability. That word is doing heavy lifting. It means revenue you can forecast, costs you can explain, and a story you can repeat quarter after quarter to strangers.

Here is the tension. The private route rewards momentum. The public route punishes surprises. A founder who lists early trades the comfort of a small board for the scrutiny of thousands of shareholders who read every filing. The upside is capital that does not dilute on someone else's timeline. The downside is that every miss becomes a headline.

My view: predictability is the real IPO readiness test, not scale. The founders who understand that will list well, whenever they choose to.

### Deeptech Wants Government to Step Up as the First Customer
12 Oct 2026 | https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/tech/newsletters/morning-dispatch/the-et-startup-awards-2026-special/articleshow/134880370.cms

Kshitij Khandelwal makes sensors that go into space, and the supply chain is getting harder. Geopolitical tensions in West Asia have lifted demand for airborne sensors and related equipment, and with that demand came higher costs and longer waits. Pixxel, the spacetech company he cofounded and serves as CTO, feels that strain directly.

On a panel at the ET Startup Awards 2026 titled Building the Hard Stuff in India, founders laid out what they need. Dheemanth Nagaraj of Agrani Labs said Indian deeptech companies need the government to become an early customer and create markets for technologies built at home.

The ask is not charity. It is a market. Global incumbents sell to governments first, and that first customer absorbs the risk of a product that is not yet proven. It funds the iteration. It writes the reference. A startup selling to private buyers alone must prove itself twice, once on the technology and again on the balance sheet.

Space is the sharpest case. Components cross borders, and borders move. A supplier in one region can become unreachable in a week, and no amount of engineering talent fixes a part that cannot be shipped.

Archana Rajaram of River Law, Priya Narsimhan of Indus Appstore, IKEA India CEO Patrik Antoni and others filled out a room where the hard questions were about procurement, not code. Laina Emmanuel of BrainSight AI and Prashanth Prakash of Accel sat on the same panel, which says something about how the conversation has shifted. The bottleneck is not ideas.

My view: deeptech in India will be built or delayed by the first purchase order. The government does not need to pick winners. It needs to buy from its own.

### Drought report: on southwest monsoon, drought in  Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh
12 Oct 2026 | https://www.thehindu.com/opinion/editorial/drought-report-on-southwest-monsoon-drought-in-karnataka-and-andhra-pradesh/article71575181.ece

A substantial drought seems to be inflicting Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh after the crucial southwest monsoon concluded with a significant deficiency in the most-affected regions. The national drought framework combines agricultural and hydrological indicators and ground-truthing studies, including before a distressed State can access additional support from the NDRF. In June-September, Karnataka reportedly suffered a close to 35% rainfall deficit. The government has since declared 217 of 240 taluks to be drought-hit with extensive damage to red gram, maize, cotton, and sugarcane cultivation. Andhra Pradesh experienced a shortfall of 53% in June-August and 388 of 688 mandals have been declared drought-hit. Horticulture crops over nearly 18,000 hectares are now at risk. Karnataka has also petitioned the Centre for ₹11,000 crore from the NDRF along with higher input subsidies. Andhra Pradesh has set aside ₹190 crore to protect horticultural crops and has directed drinking water conservation. The summer monsoon had its poorest showing since 2015 this year. There have been warnings of the effects of the strengthening El Niño, although an attribution study elucidating the strength of causal relationships in the present instance is pending.

In a previous era, drought declarations were beset by subjective claims, sometimes exaggerated to maximise state support. Since 2009, however, the Manual for Drought Management and updates to it and Finance Commission decisions have tightened the process. While States still have some incentives to expand their declarations, the Centre has also resisted releasing NDRF assistance before, including with Karnataka in 2024, while also increasing their own relief obligations. The Centre must desist from frustrating States and release support as soon as the findings of the Inter-Ministerial Central Team have been publicly verified. In addition to mitigating indebtedness among farmers and expanding access to relief beyond land ownership, the States also need more discipline vis-à-vis storing water and rationalising consumption. Andhra Pradesh and Karnataka have offered incentives and advisories, including to adopt micro-irrigation and to cultivate less water-intensive crops, but have not imposed binding limits. The Centre may also consider incentives to resist drought, even in the face of a deficient monsoon, because the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change has been warning of expanding drought-like conditions in South Asia, and broadening agricultural insurance. It must certainly not use the prospect of the more protracted, more imprudent river-interlinking project as an excuse to overlook pro-resilience aims such as sustainably using groundwater and choosing crops based on local rainfall and aquifers and availability of income support.

### Winning, differently: on the by-election results in West Bengal, Tamil Nadu and Puducherry
12 Oct 2026 | https://www.thehindu.com/opinion/editorial/winning-differently-on-the-by-election-results-in-west-bengal-tamil-nadu-and-puducherry/article71575161.ece

The by-election results in West Bengal, Tamil Nadu and Puducherry, declared on Friday, held few surprises. Barely five months after the April Assembly elections, the ruling party in each State/UT won all five bypolls. What varied was the scale of victory. In West Bengal, the BJP retained Nandigram and wrested Rejinagar, with strong vote shares of 76% and 63%. In Puducherry, the ruling All India N.R. Congress also registered an emphatic win — 61% of the votes — in Thattanchavady. Nandigram and Thattanchavady were vacated by the Chief Ministers, which partly explains the strong wins. But the contests in Tamil Nadu were closer. Dharapuram and Madurantakam fell vacant after their AIADMK MLAs resigned and defected to the ruling TVK, which renominated them. Both won their seats back with 37% and 40% of the vote, respectively, leading the DMK rival candidates by about eight percentage points. By-elections tend to favour the party in power, as voters prefer representatives with access to government. But the variance between West Bengal and Tamil Nadu reflects the nature of contest in two similarly sized States but with vastly different political cultures.

In West Bengal, Assembly victories have for decades worked as a winner-take-all mandate, which the ruling party extends to panchayat and by-elections. The pattern took shape during the Left Front’s long rule, became entrenched during the Trinamool Congress’s 15 years in power and is visible in the BJP’s tenure so far. In these contests, Opposition parties have performed far worse than in Assembly and Lok Sabha elections. In Tamil Nadu, however, a three-way contest now has kept politics competitive. The wins have helped the TVK improve its tally to 109 in the House with a sanctioned membership of 234, but the narrow margins, despite a no-holds-barred campaign by Chief Minister C. Joseph Vijay, suggest that the expected honeymoon effect did not fully materialise. The DMK, now the TVK’s principal rival, has shown that it still has a solid base despite its abrupt defeat in May. For the AIADMK, finishing third in the seats it won in May only deepens worries about its decline. Its leadership will now face sharper questions about whether it can hold on to its legislators. Four more AIADMK MLAs had joined the TVK and their seats will go to bypolls. This is a regressive turn in Tamil Nadu. MLAs are resigning barely months, sometimes days, into their terms to share in the loaves and fishes of office and the ruling party re-fields them. This defeats the spirit of the anti-defection law and overrides the Assembly election mandate. That the electorate rewarded these manoeuvres should worry anyone who is concerned about the quality of representation.

### Message of peace: On the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize
12 Oct 2026 | https://www.thehindu.com/opinion/editorial/message-of-peace-on-the-2026-nobel-peace-prize/article71571868.ece

By awarding the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize to South African jurist Navi Pillay , the Norwegian Nobel committee has sought not only to restore its credibility, tarnished by controversial decisions in the recent past, but also to strengthen the voices defending international law. Ms. Pillay served on the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda and the International Criminal Court (ICC), and chaired the UN Independent International Commission of Inquiry on the Occupied Palestinian Territory. Born in Durban in 1941 to a family of Indian Tamil origin, Ms. Pillay grew up in apartheid South Africa, opened her own legal firm in the 1960s and defended anti-apartheid political prisoners and activists on the notorious Robben Island, including Nelson Mandela. Her contributions as a jurist have shaped international criminal law. In 1998, as a part of the Rwanda war crimes tribunal, she co-authored the judgment against Jean-Paul Akayesu , a local politician, recognising that rape and sexual violence constitute acts of genocide. Last year, the Palestine commission that she led concluded that Israel had committed acts of genocide in Gaza and that Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and former Defence Minister Yoav Gallant had “incited the commission of genocide” in their speeches and statements. Compared to Barack Obama, the 2009 winner who bombed Libya two years later , or Maria Corina Machado , the 2025 winner who supported Washington’s economic strangulation of Venezuela and its illegal strikes on boats in the Caribbean, Ms. Pillay has an untarnished record of standing up for the victims of international crimes.

Ms. Pillay has been honoured at a time when the world is witnessing conflicts, which have effectively jeopardised the law-based international order. Russia’s invasion of Ukraine is a challenge to the fundamental principles of sovereignty and territorial integrity. Israel continues to bomb Gaza, Syria and Lebanon and tighten its occupation of Palestinian territories, with impunity. The U.S. under President Donald Trump — who believes he deserved a Nobel Prize — started a war on Iran on February 28 and killed its Supreme Leader, a flagrant violation of international law and norms. He has also launched a frontal attack on international institutions, including the ICC that has been placed under sanctions. What prevents the world from becoming a Hobbesian jungle where the mighty do as they please is the existence of international law and institutions that hold them accountable. The message of the 2026 Peace Prize is that the fight to strengthen those rules and institutions is part of the struggle for a more peaceful and accountable world.

### Rights and wrongs: On the fundamental right to peaceful assembly and protest
12 Oct 2026 | https://www.thehindu.com/opinion/editorial/rights-and-wrongs-on-the-fundamental-right-to-peaceful-assembly-and-protest/article71571833.ece

The Constitution of India guarantees all citizens the fundamental right to peaceful assembly and protest. The right to organise, assemble, and speak is at the core of democracy, and the curtailment of any of the fundamental rights is a curtailment of democracy itself. That being the promise of the Constitution to every citizen, regardless of gender, caste, or status, it is unsettling that the Government of India under Prime Minister Narendra Modi is brazenly knocking down those rights through police high-handedness across the country, particularly in Delhi, the capital. Youth and student organisations, such as the CJP, the AISA, SFI and AISF, had called for a peaceful protest on October 10 . Instead of ensuring peaceful assembly, as per the law of the land, the Centre ordered a countrywide swoop, where thousands of young people were taken into preventive detention and Delhi was locked down as if under martial law. Trains were cancelled and stopped, passengers waylaid, and journalists, activists, academics, and lawyers assaulted and detained, all in shocking misuse and weaponisation of the law against democracy itself. In the heart of Delhi, police personnel without identification badges have been recorded on video. What Delhi witnessed on Saturday was a repeat, in a greater degree, of the police high-handedness in July against youth protesters . Despite video evidence of male police personnel assaulting women protesters during that episode, months later, neither the government nor the Supreme Court of India has held anyone accountable for the law enforcement excesses.

On October 9, the Court had said that a blanket ban on travel or assembly could not be allowed . But that is exactly what was done in Delhi, as people, including several former Chief Ministers, senior politicians, and lawyers, were rounded up from around the city. On Sunday, the Court, in its hearing, acknowledged the urgency and seriousness of the issue, and repeated its directives . But piecemeal orders and half-hearted lamentations by the Court have had no restraining impact on a government that seems to be doubling down on its practice of branding critics as foreign agents and anti-nationals instead of coming clean on its record. The BJP government is going this far in defence of an official — the Chief Election Commissioner of India, Gyanesh Kumar. Instead of calling for accountability from the Election Commission of India, which has disenfranchised millions of Indians in a clear act of illegality, the BJP and its central and State governments are deploying the strong arm of the state to defend him. That itself suggests that Mr. Kumar’s relationship with the BJP is different from his relationship with all other parties. The Modi government should play by the rules, and not misuse power against citizens.
