  A new study in PNAS Nexus finds that Werner Heisenberg's Nazi nuclear reactor was nowhere near critical, contradicting his 1947 claim that it needed only a little more uranium. The B8 pile, built in a Haigerloch church cellar, had a neutron multiplication factor of 0.942; a reactor needs 1.0. Indigenous content averages above 54% across that programme and reaches 63% in the final lot, with only some spares, forgings and castings imported. The IAF flies 260 Su-30s and will get 12 more to replace aircraft lost in accidents; HAL estimates the fleet will need around 900 engines over its lifetime.   The essay argues that human progress has always depended less on raw genius than on the machinery and bureaucracy needed to turn ideas into reality, and that AI is about to change which of those two things is scarce. That gap is the point. Research productivity has been falling even as spending rises. Sustaining Moore's law now takes more than eighteen times as many researchers as it did in the early 1970s, and economy-wide research productivity fell by a factor of forty-one while research effort rose twenty-three-fold. The technician workforce is growing twice as fast as the scientists. ¶ The authors call the unglamorous capacity to execute "institutional intelligence," and treat it as a complement to frontier intelligence: more of one raises the value of the other. Their bet is that AI will spend most of its time on the execution side, writing code, searching literature, turning sketches into prototypes. That would let one person pursue projects that once needed a whole organization, and push creative work outside the institutions that currently gatekeep it. The thing to watch is whether that produces a wave of independent work or just a new set of bottlenecks. My view: the essay is right that execution, not ideas, is the binding constraint, and that is exactly why the incumbents with the biggest bureaucracies stand to gain most.
