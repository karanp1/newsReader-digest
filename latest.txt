IT sector sees an uptick in fresher hiring after two years of slowdown

India’s $300 billion technology and

IT services industry is seeing a revival in fresher onboarding after nearly two years of subdued hiring.

Even so, entry-level recruitment remains below mid-level hiring as demand for experienced AI talent stays strong.

Number wise: Fresher hiring, which fell to a record low of around 110,000 in FY26, is expected to rise to 115,000-135,000 in the current fiscal year, according to recruitment firm Quess Group. That implies a 4.5-22.7% increase.

The decline had been steady:

FY22: 380,000

FY23: 250,000

FY24: 155,000

FY25: 125,000

FY26: ~110,000

A rebound began in the June quarter, with TCS, Infosys and HCLTech together recruiting about 19,000 entry-level staff, Nasscom said in its September quarter report covering 29 IT services firms.

Higher bar for freshers: Experts told us the bar for entry-level talent has risen. Firms increasingly want specialised capabilities in AI, cloud, data and cybersecurity to improve revenue visibility, meet client demands and ensure deployment readiness with limited ramp-up time.

  03 Oct 2026 | https://www.tradingview.com/news/stocktwits:517fdf221094b:0-bitcoin-investors-who-bought-last-year-s-rally-are-selling-as-price-nears-their-break-even-glassnode-says/
  Bitcoin buyers from last year's rally are selling at the fastest daily pace of 2025, according to on-chain analytics firm Glassnode, because the price has fallen back to what they paid. Glassnode grouped holders by purchase date and calculated each group's average cost basis, the price at which they break even. Buyers from six to 12 months ago paid about $89,000 on average, and buyers from one to two years ago paid about $97,000. With Bitcoin trading near $84,673, both groups are underwater. The six-to-12-month cohort needs roughly a 5% gain to break even, the older group nearly 15%. ¶ That matters because underwater holders often wait for the price to return to their cost basis, then sell to exit without a loss. Each cost basis becomes a level where selling pressure builds, which helps explain why Bitcoin stalled below $85,000. Glassnode found the selling is concentrated in these 2025 buyers, while holders who bought during the decline are not moving coins. Analyst Ted Pillows says a weekly close above $87,500 is needed to confirm a rally; otherwise he sees a decent chance of a drop below $80,000. Watch that weekly close. If it fails, the cost-basis wall above will keep capping any bounce.

  03 Oct 2026 | https://indianstartupnews.com/funding/home-interior-materials-startup-gravity-raises-15-million-led-by-3one4-capital-info-edge-ventures-12613287
  Info Edge Ventures has co-led a $15 million funding round in Gravity, a Bengaluru-based startup that supplies materials for home interiors. Gravity raised the money as a mix of equity and debt, though it did not disclose the split between the two. The news was reported on 3 October 2026. ¶ Info Edge Ventures is the venture capital arm of Info Edge, the listed Indian company that owns the job portal Naukri and stakes in several internet businesses. Info Edge is the parent that most readers of this briefing will know as Naukri's owner. The $15 million round is a small cheque by Info Edge's standards, but it is a fresh deployment of capital into a private company, and it tells you where the venture arm is putting money. ¶ Gravity was founded by Saurabh Jain and Lalit Mittal, both former executives at Livspace, an Indian home interiors and renovation company. Jain was CEO for India at Livspace, where he helped scale the India business to an annualised revenue run rate of around Rs 1,500 crore. Mittal was chief business officer for India at Livspace and led its expansion across 30 cities. Earlier in his career, Jain co-founded DezignUp, a design community and marketplace that Livspace acquired in March 2015, after which he and other members of its core team joined Livspace. ¶ Gravity describes itself as a platform for India's interior materials sector. It is initially focused on kitchens and wardrobes, bringing specialist businesses onto shared technology, distribution, procurement, key-account management and operating infrastructure. Its customers are designers, architects, modular showrooms and design-and-build studios, the people who select and procure materials for interior projects. The company says it wants to simplify a supply chain that remains fragmented across dealers, distributors and suppliers that each specialise in individual product categories. The platform is meant to improve availability, fulfilment and pricing visibility for those customers. ¶ Gravity said the businesses in its initial portfolio have a combined revenue base running into several hundred crore rupees and are EBITDA positive. EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation, a rough measure of operating profit. The company did not name the businesses in that portfolio, did not give a precise revenue figure, and did not say how many businesses are included. Those are gaps a reader should note. ¶ The fresh capital will be used to strengthen technology, distribution and key-account infrastructure, expand into new categories, build the brand and support working capital as the company scales. Working capital is the cash a business needs for day-to-day operations, such as holding inventory and paying suppliers before customers pay. Gravity plans to expand into doors and windows, lighting, wall surfaces and home automation. It intends to use common technology, distribution and operating infrastructure across these categories rather than build separate systems for each business. ¶ Why is this happening now? India's interior materials market is large and split among many dealers, distributors and category-specific suppliers. A platform that aggregates demand from designers and showrooms and offers shared back-end infrastructure can, in theory, get better prices from suppliers and better availability for customers. Gravity's founders have run a scaled interiors business before, which is the main reason investors are backing them at this stage. The venture arm of Info Edge has a history of backing consumer internet and marketplace businesses, and this fits that pattern. ¶ What is still unknown is substantial. Gravity has not disclosed the equity-debt split, so the true cost of the capital and the dilution taken by existing shareholders are unclear. It has not named the businesses in its portfolio, so the claim of several hundred crore rupees in combined revenue and positive EBITDA cannot be checked. It has not said how many categories it will enter first or on what timeline. It has not said how the platform makes money, whether through commissions, subscriptions, or margin on materials sold. And it has not said how much of the $15 million is new money versus a structured debt facility from Alteria Capital, a specialist in venture debt. ¶ For Info Edge, the risks here are the usual ones for a venture bet. The investment is small relative to the parent's overall business, so a poor outcome would not move the listed company's earnings much. But the interior materials supply chain is hard to change. Dealers and distributors have long-standing relationships, credit terms and local logistics. A platform that tries to insert itself between suppliers and buyers has to offer a clear reason for both sides to switch, and it has to do so across categories with different economics. Kitchens and wardrobes are project-based and involve measurement, customisation and installation. Lighting and home automation are more standardised but have their own specialist channels. Running all of them on shared infrastructure is the bet, and it is not yet proven. ¶ There is also the question of competition. Livspace, where both founders worked, operates in the same broad space and has scale. Other players in building materials and home improvement have raised money and built distribution. Gravity's pitch is that it is a platform for materials rather than a consumer interiors brand, which puts it in a different lane, but the customers overlap. ¶ What to watch next: whether Gravity names the businesses in its portfolio and gives a precise revenue figure; whether it discloses the equity-debt split; which new category it enters first and how quickly; and whether Info Edge Ventures participates in follow-on rounds. For the listed parent, the more important numbers remain those from Naukri and its other holdings, which report separately. This deal is a signal about where the venture arm sees opportunity, not a change to the earnings picture. ¶ My view: this is a small, early-stage bet for Info Edge and it will not change the company's financial results in any meaningful way in the near term. Its value to a shareholder is as a read on the venture arm's judgment and on the founders' ability to build a platform business in a fragmented market, and that will take several years to judge.

The technician workforce is growing twice as fast as the scientists. ¶ The authors call the unglamorous capacity to execute "institutional intelligence," and treat it as a complement to frontier intelligence: more of one raises the value of the other. Their bet is that AI will spend most of its time on the execution side, writing code, searching literature, turning sketches into prototypes. That would let one person pursue projects that once needed a whole organization, and push creative work outside the institutions that currently gatekeep it. The thing to watch is whether that produces a wave of independent work or just a new set of bottlenecks. My view: the essay is right that execution, not ideas, is the binding constraint, and that is exactly why the incumbents with the biggest bureaucracies stand to gain most.
