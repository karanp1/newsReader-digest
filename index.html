Down but not out. Bitcoin's stair-step bullish trajectory is still intact

07 Oct 2026

Bitcoin fell to about $84,200 early Wednesday, down more than 2% from near $86,500 on Tuesday, but it is still trading inside the range it has held for two weeks. That matters because the drop does not break the pattern traders call a stair-step: since July, bitcoin has climbed in sudden jumps, then traded sideways for weeks, each flat stretch sitting higher than the last.

The steps are concrete. From mid-July to Aug. 18 it moved between roughly $62,000 and $67,000, then jumped 21% in three days. The next step ran from late August to mid-September between about $76,000 and $81,500, followed by a 6.6% gain from Sept. 19 to Sept. 21. Since then it has held between about $83,000 and $87,000.

The level to watch is $83,000. Vikram Subburaj of the exchange Giottus says holding it would show sellers cannot push the price back into the old band. A sustained break below $82,000 to $83,000 would signal the September breakout failed and put $80,000 to $81,500 back in play. Alex Kuptsikevich of FxPro puts support a bit higher, near $84,000. Watch $83,000; a close below it ends the staircase for now.

For a non-specialist, the useful distinction is between owning metal and owning exposure. Physical bars, coins and jewelry are one route, but most gold trading happens through exchange-traded funds, which track the gold price without anyone storing a bar. Financial advisor James Taska says rebalancing a client's gold allocation is much easier through an ETF, and that buy and sell spreads on physical gold can be wide. The trade-off is real: from 1971 to 2024, stocks averaged 10.7% annual returns against gold's 7.9%, so gold has cost long-term performance even as it hedges inflation.

My view: treat gold as portfolio insurance sized to what you can afford to underperform, not as a growth bet. Watch whether the inflation and uncertainty cited as drivers persist, because that is what the price is currently paying you to hedge against.

GE ships four F404 engines for Tejas as HAL adds Shakti line

07 Oct 2026

GE Aerospace shipped four F404-IN20 engines to Hindustan Aeronautics Limited in September 2026, the US engine maker said on 7 October. The F404-IN20 is the engine that powers the Tejas Light Combat Aircraft Mk-1A, the fighter HAL builds for the Indian Air Force. GE shipped three more F404 engines in August, so the two-month total is seven, according to Source 2 and Source 4. Source 5 says HAL has now received 10 engines in all, counting the September batch; Source 2 says the same, adding that GE's contract covers 99 engines. GE is expected to deliver more than 20 engines a year from 2027, Source 5 reports.

The engine count matters because it sets HAL's assembly pace. At least 25 Tejas airframes are finished and waiting for engines, Source 2 says. HAL has not delivered a single LCA Mk-1A fighter to the IAF. The first aircraft, ordered in February 2021, was due in March 2024. That date slipped because GE could not supply the F404-IN20 on time and because key certifications took longer than planned, according to Source 5. The 2021 contract is worth ₹48,000 crore for 83 aircraft: 73 Mk-1A fighters and 10 Mk-1 trainers, with delivery of all 83 to be completed within eight years of signing. The plan announced in 2021 was for three aircraft in the third year and 16 a year for the following five years. A second order for 97 more Mk-1A jets, signed in September 2025, brings the IAF's total to 180 aircraft worth a combined ₹1.1 lakh crore. Deliveries under the second contract are expected to start in 2027-28 and run six years.

HAL's chairman, Ravi Kota, said after completing the Tejas Mk-1 trainer contract on 18 September 2026 that he expects to deliver Mk-1A aircraft this year, according to Source 3. Jefferies, a brokerage, wrote that with 10 engines in hand there is confidence HAL can deliver 10 aircraft this year, against the brokerage's earlier assumption of five. Jefferies rates the stock Buy with a target of ₹6,800 a share; that is the brokerage's view, not the company's. The Mk-1A accounts for 43 per cent of HAL's order book, Source 3 says, and the stock has de-rated since calendar 2024 over the delays. Analysts quoted in Source 3 say the IAF accepting even one aircraft would lift confidence in HAL's execution.

Separately, HAL's Koraput division in Odisha delivered its 2,000th aero-engine on 6 October, six decades after the facility opened in 1964, Source 6 reports. The plant has built engines for the MiG-21, MiG-27, MiG-29 and Su-30MKI and has overhauled more than 9,000 engines. At the event, Defence Minister Rajnath Singh announced a second production line for Shakti engines at Koraput, costing ₹218 crore. The Shakti engine, developed with France's Safran Helicopter Engines, powers the Dhruv Advanced Light Helicopter and the Prachand Light Combat Helicopter. The new line will lift capacity to as many as 100 engines a year, with full establishment targeted for 2028-29. Singh also said HAL will raise annual AL-31FP engine capacity from 30 to 50 at a cost of ₹335 crore, supporting the Su-30MKI fleet after a 2024 government order for 240 of those engines.

The Shakti line addresses a different bottleneck: helicopters. HAL's order backlog includes 25 Dhruv Advanced Light Helicopters, 12 Light Utility Helicopters and 156 Prachand Light Combat Helicopters, plus 10 Dhruv NG helicopters ordered by Pawan Hans, according to ICICI Securities in Source 3. A further 110 LUH and 95 LCH helicopters have been planned but not yet contracted. HAL is also expanding capacity across the LCA, HTT-40 trainer and LCH lines, with about ₹12,000 crore of manufacturing investment planned by 2030, the brokerage says. HAL can build 24 LCA Mk-1As a year at its Bengaluru and Nashik lines, Source 5 reports, and aims to finish the 2021 order in three to four years.

The pressure is not only commercial. Air Chief Marshal A.P. Singh said on 3 October that the delay in inducting new fighters is hurting the IAF as its squadron strength falls, and that India needs to build up to 40 aircraft a year. He is counting on the Mk-1A, a new multi-role fighter and extra Sukhoi-30MKIs to keep the squadron count from dropping below 30, Source 5 says. Defence Minister Singh, visiting Bengaluru in September, called for realistic project timelines, warning that delays create gaps in critical defence requirements, drive cost escalation and raise the risk of technology obsolescence, Source 2 reports. The Mk-1A is meant to replace the MiG-21, retired last year after more than six decades.

What remains unconfirmed is the delivery date. Sources 2 and 5 say the IAF and HAL expect the Mk-1A to enter service by year-end, with deliveries likely to begin in December, but no aircraft has been handed over yet and the schedule has slipped before. GE has not said how many engines it will ship in the coming months beyond the 20-plus annual figure from 2027. The second Shakti line is announced, not built; full capacity is targeted for 2028-29. The 110 LUH and 95 LCH orders are planned, not signed.

The main risks are execution and dependence. HAL's fighter output depends on a single foreign engine supplier, and any further slip in F404 deliveries pushes revenue and cash collection to the right. The order book is large and the demand from the IAF is clear, but the gap between orders and deliveries is what has weighed on the stock. Certification and supply-chain steps outside HAL's control, including avionics, radars and electronic warfare systems, add further points of failure.

My view: the engine shipments are the first concrete sign that the Mk-1A bottleneck is easing, and the Shakti line is a sensible, modest fix for a separate helicopter constraint. Neither changes the fact that HAL's problem has been converting a record order book into delivered aircraft, and one quarter of engine arrivals does not settle that.

What to watch next: whether HAL hands over the first Mk-1A to the IAF by December and how many follow in the financial year; GE's monthly shipment numbers; progress on the Koraput Shakti line and the AL-31FP expansion; and any movement on the planned 110 LUH and 95 LCH contracts.

Jio Platforms targets $114 billion IPO by late October, RIL shares rise

Jio Platforms, the telecom and digital arm of Reliance Industries (RIL), is moving toward India's largest-ever initial public offering (IPO), targeting a valuation of about $114 billion (Rs 11 trillion). Reuters reported that the IPO will open on October 21 and list on October 28, raising approximately $3.8 billion (Source 5). The offering consists of up to 270 million new shares, representing about 2.93% of post-issue equity capital, per Bloomberg calculations in Source 2. At the target valuation, the shares on offer would be worth roughly $3.4 billion, exceeding the $2.9 billion raised by Hyundai Motor's Indian subsidiary in 2024, the current record. The proceeds will largely repay debt owed by Reliance Jio Infocomm, the telecom subsidiary (Source 5).

The IPO will not change RIL's control: RIL holds a 66.43% stake in Jio Platforms as of March 31, 2026, and the issue is entirely fresh shares, so no existing shares are being sold (Source 3). RIL shareholders will not receive Jio shares automatically. Instead, the draft prospectus includes a separate reservation portion for eligible RIL shareholders, defined as individuals or Hindu Undivided Families (HUFs) who hold RIL shares on the date the Red Herring Prospectus (RHP) is filed with the Registrar of Companies (Source 4). The reservation size is still a placeholder and will be disclosed in final documents. Eligible shareholders can bid up to Rs 2 lakh in this category, with allotment on a proportionate basis if demand exceeds supply. They can also apply in the regular retail category. No special price discount is mentioned in the draft (Source 4). The exact eligibility date has not been announced; buying RIL shares after the RHP filing date will not qualify for the shareholder quota.

The valuation target of $114 billion is below earlier expectations of $130 billion to $170 billion, and also below Motilal Oswal's June estimate of $115 billion to $118 billion, though it aligns with Dolat Capital's $110 billion (Source 2). At this valuation, Jio Platforms would trade at about 12 times FY28 estimated EV/EBITDA (enterprise value to earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization), according to Motilal Oswal (Source 1). The brokerage values Jio at Rs 11.2 lakh crore ($117 billion) and notes that RIL's current stock price implies a holding-company discount of 18-36% on its Jio stake, depending on the multiple assigned to Reliance Retail Ventures Ltd (RRVL). If RRVL is valued at 20 times September 2028 estimated EV/EBITDA, the implied discount is 36%; at 15 times, it is 18% (Source 1). Motilal Oswal assigns an attributable value of Rs 413 per RIL share to Jio after a 25% holding-company discount, and has a sum-of-the-parts target price of Rs 1,530 per RIL share, reiterating a 'Buy' rating (Source 1). The brokerage also has a 'Buy' on Bharti Airtel with a Rs 2,250 target, arguing that Jio's listing could narrow the valuation gap between the two telecom operators (Source 6).

Motilal Oswal expects the IPO to strengthen the case for a telecom tariff hike, as delays have weighed on both RIL and Bharti Airtel shares, which are down 15% and 23% year-to-date, respectively, compared with a 13% decline in the Nifty (Source 6). The brokerage has built in a 15% smartphone tariff hike in December 2026, which it expects to provide visibility for around 15% EBITDA compound annual growth over FY26-29. For Jio, it estimates FY28 revenue of Rs 1.69 lakh crore and EBITDA of about Rs 95,700 crore, with an EBITDA margin of 56.6% (Source 1).

On October 6, RIL shares ended 3% higher at Rs 1,219 on the BSE, and on October 7 they settled 2.49% higher at Rs 1,216, as the IPO moved closer (Source 3, Source 6). Jefferies increased its weight in RIL, according to Source 3. The IPO has drawn 19 banks, including Kotak Mahindra Capital, Morgan Stanley, BofA Securities, Axis Capital, BNP Paribas, and Citigroup (Source 2).

What remains unknown: the final RHP filing date, which determines eligibility for the shareholder quota; the exact size of the reservation portion; the price band; and the lot size (Source 4). The timeline and valuation are subject to change based on market conditions and investor demand (Source 2). The IPO market has seen volatility, with a recent stock-market rout pressuring pricing, and the National Stock Exchange reduced its offering size (Source 2). However, IPO fundraising in the July-September quarter crossed $9 billion, the highest for that period, and year-to-date proceeds exceed $13 billion (Source 2).

Risks include a holding-company discount on RIL's stake after listing, which could limit value unlocking if the discount widens. The telecom sector remains sensitive to tariff hikes, and further delays could hurt earnings. The IPO's success depends on investor appetite amid market fluctuations. Additionally, the shareholder quota details are not final, so RIL investors cannot yet act with certainty.

What to watch: the filing of the RHP, which will set the eligibility date for the shareholder reservation; the official announcement of the price band and issue dates; any updates on the tariff hike; and the final valuation and reservation size. The listing, expected by October 30, will be a key event for RIL shareholders and the broader market.

In my view, the IPO is a major step for Jio Platforms and RIL, as it provides a direct market valuation for the digital business and could unlock value for RIL shareholders. However, the actual benefit depends on the final pricing and how the market treats the holding-company discount, which is already partly factored into RIL's stock price.
