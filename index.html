HAL opens second Shakti engine line, delivers 2,000th aero-engine at Koraput

06 Oct 2026

HAL's Koraput engine division in Odisha handed over its 2,000th aero-engine on Tuesday, and Defence Minister Rajnath Singh inaugurated a second production line for the Shakti engine at the same facility. The new line costs ₹218 crore, according to officials cited by the Times of India and the Hindustan Times, and both figures appear in the Ministry of Defence statement carried by the Economic Times. HAL shares rose about 2% on Tuesday ahead of the event, Moneycontrol reported.

The Shakti is a turboshaft engine, meaning it drives a helicopter's rotor rather than a jet's thrust. HAL builds 80 Ardiden 1H1 Shakti engines a year at its Bengaluru plant today. The Koraput line lifts annual capacity to 180 engines, the officials said. The engine was co-developed with Safran of France and powers the Dhruv advanced light helicopter and the Prachand light combat helicopter.

The demand case behind the extra capacity is a Ministry of Defence order worth ₹62,700 crore for 156 Prachand helicopters, split as 66 for the Indian Air Force and 90 for the Army. Deliveries start in the third year and run five years, per the Economic Times. The government wants more than 65% of the Prachand contract executed with Indian content, with over 250 domestic suppliers, mostly small and medium firms, involved. The Prachand is built for high-altitude operations above 5,000 metres.

The 2,000th engine itself was an AL-31FP, the powerplant for the Sukhoi-30MKI fighter. Koraput has made engines under Russian licence since 1964, starting with the R11-F2/F2S for the MiG-21FL, then the R25 for the MiG-21BIS, the R29B for the MiG-27M, and from 2007-08 the RD-33 Series 3 for the MiG-29. AL-31FP production began in 2004. The division has overhauled more than 7,700 aero-engines over five decades.

In September 2024 the Defence Ministry signed a ₹26,000 crore contract with HAL for 240 AL-31FP engines, to be built at Koraput at 30 a year over eight years. Indigenous content averages above 54% across that programme and reaches 63% in the final lot, with only some spares, forgings and castings imported. The IAF flies 260 Su-30s and will get 12 more to replace aircraft lost in accidents; HAL estimates the fleet will need around 900 engines over its lifetime. That is a long revenue tail, though it is spread over many years.

Separately, HAL has been finishing the original Tejas order. The last two twin-seat trainers from the December 2010 contract for 40 Tejas Mk1 jets, designated SPT7 and SPT8, have been fitted with Israeli EL/M-2052 AESA radars and air-to-air refuelling probes, according to Indian Defence Research Wing. AESA means an electronically scanned radar with no moving antenna. Earlier trainers carried the older mechanically steered EL/M-2032. The 2010 order was split into 20 Initial Operational Clearance and 20 Final Operational Clearance aircraft, including four twin-seaters. With these two done, HAL says it can focus on the Mk1A line.

The Mk1A pipeline is large. A 2021 contract covered 83 aircraft, 73 single-seat fighters and 10 twin-seat trainers. A 2025 follow-on added 97 jets, including 29 trainers. Those 29 trainers must carry the domestically developed Uttam AESA radar and the Swayam Raksha Kavach electronic warfare suite as standard, replacing imported Israeli equipment. The Uttam has reached production integration, per IDRW.

What is not confirmed: no source gives the value of the Shakti line's output, the delivery schedule for the 180-engine capacity, or how much of the Shakti's content is Indian. The 2,000th engine handover is a cumulative milestone, not a new order. The reports also do not say when the first Prachand airframes will be ready to receive engines, so the link between the new line and the ₹62,700 crore contract is a capacity claim, not a booked revenue figure.

The risks sit in execution. The Tejas Mk1A programme is behind schedule, the Economic Times notes, partly because GE Aerospace of the United States has been slow to supply F404-IN20 engines. HAL is negotiating with GE for joint production of the F414 engine in India for the LCA Mk-2, and India is talking to Safran and Rolls Royce about domestic engine work. Licensed manufacture with rising local content is not the same as designing an engine from scratch; India still lacks high-temperature materials, turbine, combustion and control expertise, and testing and certification capacity, according to defence experts quoted by the Economic Times. HAL and Safran signed a final contract in August through their joint venture SAFHAL for the Aravalli, a 3,500-4,000 shaft horsepower turboshaft, which retired IAF official N K Samal called a bridge between licensed manufacturing and joint development.

My view: the Shakti line is a real capacity addition tied to a contract already signed, and the engine business is becoming a steadier earnings base than airframe assembly, which depends on outside suppliers. The 2,000th engine is a ceremony, not a change in the numbers. What matters over the next year is whether the Prachand deliveries start on time and whether GE's F404 shipments stop holding up Tejas Mk1A handovers.

Watch the first Prachand delivery dates against the third-year start, the monthly F404-IN20 arrival rate from GE, progress on the SAFHAL Aravalli contract, and any order confirmation for the 12 replacement Su-30s.

Digital services currently contribute 15% of revenue, but Jio targets a 60:40 connectivity-to-digital mix over the next few years. In enterprise, Jio plans to offer connectivity plus services like CRM and managed Wi-Fi to tap India's $52 billion ICT market, where telecom operators currently hold only 5-7% share. Internationally, Jio is exploring partnerships in Europe, East Asia, and Africa to deploy its technology stack without heavy capital investment.

The IPO market is active, with nearly 250 companies in the pipeline, which could divert investor attention.

What to watch next: the filing of the final prospectus, the official price band announcement, the anchor investor list, and the subscription levels on October 21-23. In my view, this IPO is a significant event for Jio Platforms, as it provides capital to reduce debt and positions the company to invest in growth areas like enterprise and digital services. The essay argues that human progress has always depended less on raw genius than on the machinery and bureaucracy needed to turn ideas into reality, and that AI is about to change which of those two things is scarce. That gap is the point. Research productivity has been falling even as spending rises. Sustaining Moore's law now takes more than eighteen times as many researchers as it did in the early 1970s, and economy-wide research productivity fell by a factor of forty-one while research effort rose twenty-three-fold. The technician workforce is growing twice as fast as the scientists.

The authors call the unglamorous capacity to execute "institutional intelligence," and treat it as a complement to frontier intelligence: more of one raises the value of the other. Their bet is that AI will spend most of its time on the execution side, writing code, searching literature, turning sketches into prototypes. The thing to watch is whether that produces a wave of independent work or just a new set of bottlenecks. My view: the essay is right that execution, not ideas, is the binding constraint, and that is exactly why the incumbents with the biggest bureaucracies stand to gain most.
