OpenAILegalOn halves Codex costs while maintaining development speed

08 Oct 2026

LegalOn gave every engineer the most powerful model it had. That worked, right up until the invoice arrived.

The company builds AI for legal work, and it had wired Codex into how its developers write software. GPT-5.5 in Fast mode, unlimited, across design, implementation and daily work. Then the math caught up. Unlimited use of a top model cannot survive an annual budget, and a blanket ban would have thrown away the gains. So the company did something less obvious than switching tools. It taught people to choose.

An internal group, the AI-powered Development CoE, wrote model-selection guidelines and tested which model fit which job. The lightest, GPT-6 Luna, handles implementation with clear requirements. GPT-6.1 Sol covers standard design, data analysis and documents. GPT-6 Astra, the strongest, gets architecture and hard judgment calls. Engineers pick for themselves now. Managers spread the findings; the CoE watches usage and adjusts.

The controls matter as much as the guidelines. Fast mode is off by default, granted only on request. Monthly limits sit at department and individual level. Mature businesses were asked to find roughly 20% more efficiency. New businesses got generous budgets on purpose, because speed mattered more than savings there. Estimated daily costs fell about 65% against GPT-5.5, and development speed held, helped by running tasks in parallel.

Here is the part worth sitting with. LegalOn's own engineering manager, Yuta Tokitake, says faster development is nearly a given now. What he cannot yet see is whether it produces anything a customer values. So the company is building a metric around each feature release, tying the customer value it delivers to the AI cost spent on it. Today you can price a task. You cannot price a feature.

My view: cost control is the easy half of this problem, and most teams will stop there. The harder discipline is refusing to call cheaper AI a win until someone can name what the customer got.

OpenAIDisrupting AI-enabled “false front” operations

08 Oct 2026

The Iranian operation ran seven journalists. None of them existed. They pitched long-form articles to small and mid-sized outlets around the world, and the outlets published them, because the pitches were good and the deadlines were real. That is the whole trick. Not a bot army shouting into a void. A byline.

OpenAI banned two of these operations, one from Russia and one from Iran, and published the details. The Iranian one built a stable of seven fake "journalist" personas and fed articles to real publications. The Russian one co-opted unwitting people in Latin America into running a think tank on the ground, a self-described research platform called the Social Research Center, steered through a fake persona named Mia Clark. The operators prompted in Russian, connected over VPNs, and used the models for the unglamorous work: internal reports, social media comment batches, fake leaked documents and audio scripts.

Here is the part worth sitting with. On the IO Breakout Scale, which rates influence operations from 1 to 6, the Russian operation scored a 5. The first Category 5 operation OpenAI has disrupted since it started reporting. The Iranian one hit 4. Both landed content in mainstream outlets, not just social feeds. Across the 30 covert operations OpenAI has exposed in two and a half years, that pattern holds: the ones that get into real media reach furthest.

The AI did not invent the playbook. Alice Donovan, a fake journalist fronting for Russian military intelligence, placed articles in Western outlets in 2016 and 2017. PeaceData co-opted real freelancers in 2020. What AI adds is throughput. More personas, more languages, cleaner prose, faster pitches, and internal reports that oversell the operation's own success.

My view: exposure is the defense that actually works. Both Alice Donovan and PeaceData went quiet once they were named. These operations borrow credibility from real editors and real audiences, and that borrowed credibility burns the moment someone writes it down.

AnthropicIntroducing the Anthropic Cyber Mission

08 Oct 2026

Anthropic is not selling security software. It is buying a seat at the table where the fixes get made.

The systems that worry defenders most are the ones nobody can touch. Power grids, water utilities, factories and rail networks run on controllers and industrial networks built to last decades. The industry calls this operational technology. You cannot take a substation offline to install a patch, so a known flaw can sit there for years. The equipment is proprietary, the changes are risky, and one bad move can shut a plant.

Anthropic's answer is the Critical Infrastructure Defense Program. It puts frontier Claude models, on-site engineers and threat research inside the small set of providers that operators already trust to touch these systems. The founding list is eleven names, among them Accenture, CrowdStrike, Deloitte, Dragos, Hitachi, Palo Alto Networks, PwC and Rockwell Automation. Consulting firms that run security programs. Security vendors that watch the networks. Manufacturers that build and patch the equipment itself. Anthropic supplies the model and the people. The partners supply the access.

The second half of the mission is quieter and maybe larger. OSS Scanner gives open-source projects free, regular security scans from Anthropic's strongest models. That code sits under most of the software everyone runs, maintained by small teams with no budget. Anthropic's own account of Project Glasswing is the honest part: finding vulnerabilities is now easy. Verifying, ranking and fixing them is not. A scanner that produces a thousand findings and no patches just moves the bottleneck.

The company frames the whole thing as a race. Frontier models can be turned on these systems, and state-backed adversaries have spent years getting footholds. Anthropic wants its models pointed the other way first.

My view: the model is not the product here. Access is. Anthropic is trading compute for the trust of the firms that decide what gets patched on a running grid.

Open Claw NewsletterNo More Grocery List

12 Oct 2026

The grocery list is a small piece of software that runs in your head. It knows the household: the milk that runs out on Thursdays, the brand of pasta the kids will actually eat, the budget that tightens at month's end. Kroger wants that job.

At GroceryShop 2026 in Las Vegas, Chief Digital Officer Yael Cosset laid out a three-stage ladder: assisted, augmented, autonomous. Most companies stop at the first rung and call it a strategy. Kroger named the top rung and then said something rarer. "The goal is not autonomy everywhere," Cosset said. The level of AI should match the risk, the repeatability, and how much human judgment a decision needs.

That sounds modest until you see what sits underneath. Kroger's loyalty program has spent years recording what millions of households buy, week after week. Demand forecasting, personalization, operational models. The company is now wiring those proprietary models together with third-party frontier models and shared infrastructure, so agents can be orchestrated across workflows rather than bolted on one at a time.

The customer-facing piece is already here. An AI Shopping Assistant plans meals, finds recipes, and builds a cart around budget and dietary needs. Conversational search does the finding. More than 400,000 associates are getting AI literacy training, and Kroger is building agents for frontline staff to filter dozens of daily notifications down to the few that matter.

Then the frontier. Cosset described grocery as a managed service: instead of writing a list, a customer shares goals and preferences, and agents handle planning, shopping and fulfillment. The list stops being a document and becomes an outcome.

Here is the catch. That model only works if the customer hands over the information that makes the list possible. Kroger's advantage is what it already knows about shopping patterns and product availability. The ask is trust, at a scale no coupon program ever demanded.

My view: the list was never the product. It was the last place a shopper held full control. Kroger is betting enough people will trade it for the outcome.

Open Claw NewsletterEnterprise AI Hits Orchestration Gap

12 Oct 2026

The average large American company now runs 183 AI tools. Not pilots. Tools in production, scattered across departments, each one bought by someone with a budget and a problem.

Here is the catch. Eighty-five percent of those companies have no system connecting them. The tools answer their own questions and keep their own notes. Nothing carries across.

The survey, from WSJ Intelligence and the agency Code and Theory, asked 801 executives at companies with at least $500 million in revenue. Ninety-four percent said better orchestration, meaning one layer that routes work and data between tools, is essential to growth. Almost none have built it. Only 15% describe their AI investments as a unified system with shared intelligence. Two-thirds have not even issued a request for proposals to buy one.

The stall is not technical. Executives blame siloed mindsets and culture first, at 61%, then fragmented data models, then the missing operating layer. Notice who is supposed to own the fix. Forty-two percent of CEOs say the job is theirs. Fifty-seven percent of technology leaders say it is theirs. One in four companies concede that no one is accountable. When two executives each believe they hold the wheel, the car tends to drift.

The cost shows up as waste. Three in five executives say tool sprawl has frozen strategic execution. Two-thirds keep discovering AI investments they already paid for somewhere else. And 78% say customer-facing channels still run on no shared data model, which means the same customer can look like three different people to three different products.

My view: the orchestration layer will not be bought as software. It will be claimed as a job. The company that names one owner, with the authority to kill redundant tools and force shared data, will outrun rivals holding better models and worse wiring.

Open Claw NewsletterCheap Labor Loses Its Edge

12 Oct 2026

For twenty years, a young person in Manila or Nairobi could get a desk job by answering a phone. The work was scripted, repetitive, and paid a fraction of what the same job paid in Ohio. It was also a ladder. That ladder is being pulled up.

Revelio Labs tracks headcount across global call centers. Since December 2023, employment is down 5.3%. In the seventy quarter-end readings since 2009, headcount fell year over year only eight times. All eight came in the last eight quarters, and the most recent drop is the steepest yet.

The operators are not hiding why. TP, one of the largest global call center companies, is chasing €150 to 170 million in annual savings from AI and cost cuts. TaskUs's chief executive has said agentic AI, software that completes tasks on its own, could take 25 to 50% out of customer support costs. The work was always high-volume and scripted. That is exactly the profile AI does well.

The decline moved down the income ladder. It started in high-income countries, spread to middle-income ones, and by the fourth quarter of 2025 every market was shrinking. China and Nigeria still grew, but they are exceptions.

Here is the part that should worry policymakers. The industry is not shedding workers. Hiring simply stopped. Entries into call center jobs fell from 25.1 to 17.2 per 100 workers since November 2022, while exits fell too, from 22.3 to 18.5. Entries dropped below exits. The door closed quietly.

And the workers who do leave mostly do not reach the good jobs. Half move sideways, into another customer service or sales role. Only 10.8% land in technical support, customer success, or software and data, the roles growing 2.6% to 8.9% a year and paying 31% to more than double the call center rate.

My view: the office economy in these countries is still expanding, and that is real. But the entry rung is gone, and the rungs above it are not catching the people who used to climb.

Bending Spoons bought AOL, Vimeo, WeTransfer and Evernote. Then it raised prices, cut staff, and watched revenue per customer climb. That is the playbook. The new part is what feeds it.

The company writes at least 90% of its code with AI. It uses the same tools to design products, tune how each one charges, and screen job applicants. A firm that once needed a full engineering floor to overhaul a tired app now needs a much smaller one. The work that used to take a year takes months. So the math on any acquisition gets better, and the appetite grows.

Then there is the timing. Earlier this year, investors panicked that AI would gut software-as-a-service companies, and their valuations fell. Bending Spoons was buying while the aisle was empty. Those prices have mostly recovered, but the company's chief executive calls them reasonable now. Cheap inputs, faster rebuilds. The two advantages compound.

Look at the shopping list. In its June IPO filing, Bending Spoons named more than 1,000 digital businesses as potential targets, together representing close to $400 billion in estimated 2025 revenue. It raised about $953.9 million when it listed on Nasdaq on July 1. By September 10 it was preparing to pay $1.355 billion for Miro, the workplace collaboration platform, promising to invest in performance, reliability and functionality once the deal closes.

Here is the catch. AI lowers the cost of fixing software, so it also lowers the cost of building it. The same tailwind that lets Bending Spoons revive AOL lets someone else build AOL's replacement. Its edge is not the model. It is the discipline of buying low and charging more.

My view: the durable moat is the portfolio and the pricing power, not the code generation. AI just makes the treadmill spin faster.

Open Claw NewsletterAston Martin Turns AI Into Racecraft

12 Oct 2026

Forty radio channels talk at once during a Formula One race. Aston Martin now has software listening to all of them.

Every team is allowed to tune into its rivals' radio. Almost none can use what they hear in time. At the 2026 Monaco Grand Prix, teams spoke more than 380,000 words across 50 hours of audio and 21,000 messages, per CoreWeave. Answering a simple question about a rival's tire wear used to mean pulling an engineer off other work to dig through transcripts. Minutes passed. The race did not wait.

The new system transcribes and sorts each message within five seconds. A race engineer can ask how a competitor is finding a tire compound and get an answer mid-race. CoreWeave built it on site from October 2025, covering 22 competitor channels plus the team's own. The model went through 75 iterations and trained on more than 3,000 labeled samples of real race audio. It ran as a test at the last two races of 2025 and went live for 2026. CoreWeave's numbers are self-reported, so treat them as claims, not audited results.

Money shapes the rest. Formula One caps car-performance spending at $215 million for 2026, and the FIA rations wind tunnel time. So Aston Martin runs models trained on a smaller set of high-fidelity airflow simulations to estimate design changes without running every full simulation. Falling compute costs help. "Every 12 to 18 months, the crunch gets cheaper," the team's CIO told TechInformed. The savings go back into engineering.

Three AI partners arrived in 18 months: CoreWeave for cloud and race data, Cohere for generative AI across engineering and operations, Cognition for autonomous coding agents. AI and machine learning brands are now 4 of the top 15 new sponsorship investors in the sport.

My view: the edge in F1 is shifting from who can afford the fastest car to who can turn the flood of race data into a decision before the next corner.

Open Claw NewsletterAgents Threaten Cheap Bank Deposits

12 Oct 2026

A checking account paying 0.1% is not a product. It is a habit. Banks have spent decades collecting on it.

Torsten Sløk, chief economist at Apollo Global Management, put a number on the habit in a Sept. 27 note. If households let AI agents hunt for yield, banks lose the cheap deposits that fund their loans. The gap is not subtle. The national average on checking is 0.1%. High-yield accounts pay 3.3% to 5%. That is real money sitting still, and the only reason it stays is that nobody wants to spend a Sunday afternoon moving it.

Meta launched Muse on Sept. 8. Ten days later it passed ChatGPT to top Apple's App Store. Muse connects to bank accounts through Plaid, the network that lets apps read balances, transactions, holdings and mortgage data. Plaid's announcement does not say Muse can move money between banks. Reading is not moving. That distinction is doing a lot of work right now.

The infrastructure to move it already exists. Rivo, a San Francisco FinTech, raised $3.1 million in August to watch a customer's cash flow and sweep idle balances into Treasury bills, pulling the money back before bills land. Raisin has gathered more than $80 billion and placed it across 250-plus partner banks. The deposits stay in the banking system. They just stop belonging to one bank.

Citi's Andrew Coombs expects the shift to come slowly. Trust, reliability, transparency and regulation will hold third-party agents back, he argued, and banks will build the tools into their own apps instead. JPMorgan is already there. Jamie Dimon told shareholders that in 2026 clients will move money between checking and higher-yield brokerage products automatically, because "our Smart Cash capability will do it for them."

Notice who is racing whom. The banks are not defending the 0.1%. They are trying to be the agent that moves it.

My view: the deposit franchise was never a moat. It was a switching cost, and switching costs die when someone else does the switching.

Open Claw NewsletterNadella Wants an AI Emergency Brake

12 Oct 2026

The most powerful buyer of AI in the world just asked for a kill switch. Satya Nadella, writing on X, says we can no longer treat advanced models as "nested black boxes" whose answers we simply accept or reject. He wants them contained, observed, and forced to leave what he calls tamper-proof human readable evidence.

The sharpest line is about containment. Assume a model is compromised, he says, and contain it from the start. An authorized person should always be able to pause or shut down a model mid-task. He calls it an emergency brake, and adds that more advanced models will need more advanced containment, standardized across the industry.

The rest of his list is familiar. Disclose incidents quickly. Submit to independent audits. Verify the data. Anyone who has watched AI policy for two years has heard versions of this. The brake is where he moves ahead of his peers. Most labs talk about alignment, which is a research problem. A brake is an operational one. It assumes the model will fail, and asks who holds the handle.

That framing matters for anyone shipping AI products. Containment is an architecture decision, not a policy one. A system you can pause mid-task has to be built with checkpoints, state you can freeze, and an owner with the authority to stop it. Retrofitting that after launch is expensive. Building it in changes what the product can do.

One tell: Nadella calls the technology "superintelligence" throughout. That word does work. It justifies the brake. It also flatters the thing being braked.

My view: the emergency brake is the right ask, and the hardest part is not the technology. It is naming the person authorized to pull it, and giving them the nerve to do it while the model is still talking.

Vidit Aatrey and Lalit Keshre listed their companies a month apart late last year. On a panel at the ET Startup Awards 2026, moderated by ETtech editor Samidha Sharma, they disagreed about why founders are rushing to the public markets earlier than before.

Aatrey sees a practical shift. In the US, small IPOs are rare. In India, both small and large listings happen. Founders look at that and ask a direct question: can I short-circuit the private fundraising and go public instead? Public markets, in this reading, are not a graduation ceremony. They are an alternative route.

Keshre put the question differently. Capital, he said, is available where capital is needed. The problem is valuation. At a certain stage, valuing an asset is very tough. In private markets, two parties decide. In public markets, a lot more parties do. Groww listed in November, and Meesho followed, two decade-old companies choosing the same door for different reasons.

Both agreed on the precondition. Startups should go public only when their businesses have achieved a degree of predictability. That word is doing heavy lifting. It means revenue you can forecast, costs you can explain, and a story you can repeat quarter after quarter to strangers.

Here is the tension. The private route rewards momentum. The public route punishes surprises. A founder who lists early trades the comfort of a small board for the scrutiny of thousands of shareholders who read every filing. The upside is capital that does not dilute on someone else's timeline. The downside is that every miss becomes a headline.

My view: predictability is the real IPO readiness test, not scale. The founders who understand that will list well, whenever they choose to.
