ETSA 2026: Consumer brands poised for bigger exits as strategic acquisitions pick up: Sauce’s Manu Chandra

Manu Chandra, founder, Sauce

India’s new-age consumer brands

are entering a phase offering larger exits to investors , with strategic acquisitions expected to become more frequent as companies scale and take market share from established players, according to Manu Chandra, founder and managing partner of consumer-focused VC firm Sauce.

Driving the news: “Five years from now, we will see many more smaller niche brands getting developed and many more frequent midsize acquisitions happening,” Chandra told ET in an interview.

Chandra won the Midas Touch honour for best investor at The ET Startup Awards 2026, with the jury recognising his track record of backing companies early and delivering significant exits.

Innovist exit: In June, Sauce recorded one of its biggest exits yet, pocketing an estimated Rs 500-550 crore from L’Oréal’s acquisition of Innovist, in which it had invested Rs 50–55 crore. Chandra, a former investor at British PE firm 3i Group, founded Sauce in 2019 with a modest Rs 60 crore fund.

“If you think about M&A, many a time it makes a lot of sense for a company to acquire the synergistic or complementary strengths of the acquirer and pass on their strengths to them,” Chandra said.

Raising the stakes: The bar for an attractive consumer business has risen sharply. A company hitting Rs 100 crore revenue “does not excite us anymore,” Chandra said. He expects leading brands to scale considerably, with companies generating Rs 1,500-2,000 crore in revenue potentially delivering valuations of Rs 10,000-12,000 crore.

SEC Clears 3x Leveraged Bitcoin and Ethereum Funds for Trading

05 Oct 2026

The SEC approved a Cboe rule change on October 2 allowing six funds from Volatility Shares to list on a U.S. exchange, each targeting three times the daily price move of Bitcoin, Ethereum, gold, silver, crude oil, or natural gas. If Bitcoin futures rise 2% in a day, the fund aims to gain 6%; if they fall 2%, it aims to lose 6%. The funds get that exposure mainly through futures, contracts to buy or sell an asset at a set price later.

The word "daily" matters. These funds reset every day, so the 3x promise applies one day at a time. Over longer stretches, returns drift far from three times the asset's move. If Bitcoin futures drop 10% Monday and rise 10% Tuesday, the asset ends down 1% while the fund ends down 9%. The SEC and FINRA have warned investors about exactly this.

The approval follows SEC pushback on leverage: warning letters to nine issuers in December 2025 over products above 2x, and a March 2026 request to avoid 5x. The order sets no launch date; shares cannot trade until each fund's registration takes effect. Watch whether these funds attract real money or stay a niche trading tool, because daily-reset leverage punishes anyone who holds too long.

Bernstein predicts Bitcoin will reach $125,000 by December 31, 2026, roughly a 45% climb from its current $86,093. Michaël van de Poppe expects a new all-time high by January 2027, which would take a similar gain. Both calls land in the same range, and both require a rally that Bitcoin has pulled off before: it has gained 45% or more in seven of the past 13 fourth quarters, with a median Q4 return of 48%.

The context is the four-year halving cycle. Every four years, the reward miners get for processing transactions is cut, slowing the supply of new coins. Prices have historically peaked about a year or more after each halving, and Bernstein reads the current downturn, Bitcoin sits 32% below its October 2025 record of $126,080, as a pause rather than the end of the cycle. Van de Poppe is less comfortable in the near term, warning a 20% to 40% correction could drag Bitcoin as low as $51,700 before any new high. Watch whether the halving pattern holds. I think the cycle story is doing most of the work in both forecasts, and a 45% move in a quarter is a bet, not a base case.

HAL closes Tejas Mk1 order, adds Shakti engine line, buys out CAE training venture

05 Oct 2026

Hindustan Aeronautics Ltd packed three separate developments into the first week of October 2026: the last two Tejas Mk1 trainers left the legacy production line with new radars, a second Shakti helicopter engine line was announced at Koraput, and HAL agreed to buy CAE Canada's half of a helicopter training joint venture.

The Tejas news concerns SPT7 and SPT8, the final two twin-seat trainers from the original 40-aircraft Mk1 order placed in December 2010. That contract split into 20 Initial Operational Clearance (IOC) aircraft and 20 Final Operational Clearance (FOC) aircraft, of which four were twin-seat trainers. HAL fitted these last two with the Israeli EL/M-2052 AESA radar and air-to-air refuelling probes. AESA stands for active electronically scanned array, a radar that steers its beam electronically rather than by moving a dish. Earlier Tejas trainers carried the older mechanically steered EL/M-2032. The point of the upgrade is pilot transition: the Indian Air Force wants trainees to meet the sensor setup they will use in the operational Tejas Mk1A combat variant. With these two airframes done, HAL says it is closing the 2010 order and moving its Tejas effort entirely to the Mk1A line.

The Mk1A pipeline is where the volume sits. In 2021 the Ministry of Defence signed for 83 aircraft, 73 single-seat fighters and 10 twin-seat trainers. A follow-on order in 2025 added 97 more, including 29 twin-seat trainers. Those 29 are to carry the domestically developed Uttam AESA radar and the Swayam Raksha Kavach electronic warfare suite as standard, replacing the imported Israeli equipment used on the current FOC trainers. The article describes Uttam as having matured into production integration, though it gives no delivery schedule for the 97-aircraft batch.

At Koraput in Odisha, Defence Minister Rajnath Singh is due to lay the foundation stone on Tuesday for a second Shakti engine production line, at an investment of ₹218 crore. The same event marks delivery of HAL Koraput's 2,000th aero-engine. Shakti is an Indo-French programme between HAL and Safran Helicopter Engines; the new line will build the Indian version of the Ardiden 1H1 turboshaft engine. A turboshaft is a gas turbine that drives a helicopter rotor. The stated demand driver is helicopter orders: the MoD has signed ₹62,700 crore of contracts with HAL for 156 Prachand light combat helicopters, 66 for the Air Force and 90 for the Army. Deliveries are to begin in the third year and run over five years. The government targets more than 65 per cent indigenous content in that contract, with more than 250 domestic suppliers, mostly small and medium enterprises. The Prachand is designed for operations above 5,000 metres.

Koraput's history matters for judging the new line. The division opened in April 1964 building R11-F2/F2S turbojets for the MiG-21FL under licence, then R25 and R29B engines for the MiG-21BIS and MiG-27M, and from 2007-08 the RD-33 Series 3 for the MiG-29. A second facility there builds the AL-31FP for the Su-30MKI, under a 2004 Russian licence. In September 2024 the ministry signed a ₹26,000 crore contract for 240 AL-31FP engines, to be built at Koraput at 30 a year over eight years, with indigenous content rising to 63 per cent by the end of deliveries and averaging above 54 per cent. The division says it has built 2,000 engines and overhauled more than 7,700 over five decades. Separately, HAL and Safran signed a final contract in August 2026 through their joint venture SAFHAL Helicopter Engines for design, development, manufacture, supply and lifecycle support of the Aravalli, a 3,500-4,000 shaft horsepower turboshaft.

On the training side, HAL has agreed to acquire CAE Canada's 50 per cent stake in HATSOFF Helicopter Training, comprising 38,404,205 equity shares of ₹10 face value each, at nil consideration. HATSOFF runs military and civil helicopter pilot training on simulators and is currently a 50:50 joint venture. After transfer it becomes a wholly owned HAL subsidiary. HAL expects the deal to close within about 60 days of the share transfer agreement. HAL shares traded at ₹4,648.90 on the BSE, up 0.74 per cent, giving a market capitalisation of ₹310,712.87 crore. The stock's 52-week high is ₹5,149.90, set on 17 August 2026, and its low ₹3,479.20 on 30 March 2026. Promoters hold 71.64 per cent.

What is not established: the cost, capacity, construction timeline and commissioning date of the second Shakti line. Source 6 says the report it draws on does not provide those details, and Source 2 gives only the ₹218 crore investment figure. No delivery schedule is given for the 97-aircraft Mk1A order, and no first-flight or handover date appears for SPT7 and SPT8. The nil-consideration transfer raises an obvious question about why CAE is exiting for nothing, which none of the articles answers.

The risks run along familiar lines. Engine work under licence is not the same as designing an engine from scratch. N K Samal, a retired Indian Air Force official, is quoted saying India still lacks expertise in high-temperature materials, turbine technology, combustion systems and engine controls, plus testing and certification, and that the HAL-Safran Aravalli programme is expected to bridge licensed manufacturing and genuine joint development. The Tejas programme carries its own transition risk: the trainers now flying carry imported radars, while the next 29 trainers must carry the indigenous Uttam and Swayam Raksha Kavach. Any slip in those systems lands on the Mk1A schedule. The Prachand order is large but back-loaded, with deliveries starting in year three, so the revenue arrives well after the ₹218 crore is spent.

My view: the Tejas milestone is mostly symbolic, the end of a 2010 order, while the Shakti line and the Prachand contract are the parts that touch future revenue. The buyout of CAE is small in money terms but removes a partner from a training business HAL evidently wants to control.

Watch the foundation event for the Shakti line's capacity and commissioning date, any official word on Uttam radar deliveries for the 29 new trainers, and confirmation that the HATSOFF share transfer completes inside the stated 60 days.

Nuvama Research expects Reliance Jio's profit after tax to grow at an 18% compound annual rate between FY26 and FY30.

Ownership is concentrated. Reliance Industries holds about 66.4%. Meta's affiliate Jaadhu Holdings has 9.98% and Google International 7.73%, from investments of Rs 43,574 crore and Rs 33,737 crore respectively in 2020. Other investors, including Silver Lake, Vista Equity Partners, General Atlantic, KKR, Mubadala, Abu Dhabi Investment Authority, TPG, L Catterton, Intel Capital and Qualcomm Ventures, hold roughly 15.2% after investing about Rs 74,745 crore. Institutional and other large investors together own nearly 30.9%, according to the draft prospectus.

The IPO has moved quickly through the regulatory process. Jio Platforms filed draft papers in June, and the Securities and Exchange Board of India issued final observations on August 28. The listing would be the first public offering from the Reliance group since 2008, and the first consumer-focused IPO within the conglomerate. It arrives in an active primary market: more than two dozen IPOs have been announced or launched since July 1, compared with 28 in the first half of 2026.

Several details remain unconfirmed. The company has not commented publicly on the timetable, price band or valuation. The $3.8 billion figure and the October 21 date come from unnamed sources, not from Jio Platforms. The final size could shift with the price band and demand. The $143-146 billion valuation is described as a base figure based on interest from overseas roadshows, and the wider $130-170 billion range shows how unsettled the number is. The exact use of the remaining proceeds beyond the Rs 27,500 crore debt prepayment is not specified.

The main risks sit in the business itself. Telecom is capital-intensive, and Jio's 5G buildout and fixed wireless expansion require continued spending. Competition from Bharti Airtel and Vodafone Idea persists, though Jio's market share is the largest. The IPO's success depends on demand at the price band, and a large anchor round can absorb supply that might otherwise go to retail investors. The debt repayment reduces leverage at the telecom subsidiary but does not by itself change the competitive position.

My view: this is a material event for the company because it converts a private, Reliance-controlled asset into a listed one with a public shareholder base, and the debt prepayment at Reliance Jio Infocomm strengthens the balance sheet of the unit that generates most of the cash flow. The scale of the offer, at roughly 2.9% of post-issue capital, means control stays firmly with Reliance Industries.

What to watch next: the red herring prospectus in the week starting October 12, which should confirm the price band, the final issue size and the use of proceeds; the anchor round on October 19; subscription figures on October 21-23; and the listing on October 28, when the market will put its own value on the business.

Flipkart buys minority stake in B2B travel platform TravClan

05 Oct 2026

Flipkart has taken a minority stake in TravClan, a Delhi-based business-to-business travel technology platform, the company said on 5 October 2026. B2B means the platform sells to travel agents rather than to consumers directly. Neither side disclosed the amount paid or the size of the stake. The deal runs through Flipkart itself, not its venture arm Flipkart Ventures, which made separate investments in two AI startups a week earlier.

TravClan was founded in 2018 by Arun Bagaria, Chirag Agrawal and Ashish Thapliyal. It lists travel suppliers directly, covering domestic and international destinations, and more than 15,000 travel agents use its technology to customise and book holiday packages, hotels and activities in real time. The company also runs its own ground teams and offers support around the clock. Its shareholders include angel investors Vikram Chopra and Mehul Agrawal, cofounders of used-car platform Cars24, Trip.com's Travis Katz, and institutional investor Leo Capital. Flipkart's own cap table, by contrast, is not part of this transaction.

What Flipkart gets is access to that supplier network and those destination operations. The company says the investment will expand international holiday packages sold through Cleartrip and Flipkart Travel, and deepen on-ground assistance for customers who buy them. Cleartrip is the online travel agency Flipkart acquired in 2021. Flipkart Travel is its in-house travel business, which has moved beyond flight bookings into bus and hotel bookings in recent years. Cleartrip later added corporate travel.

Nishant Verman, Flipkart's senior vice president of corporate development and partnerships, framed the rationale around changing customer expectations. "Travellers today are looking for more than a flight or hotel booking," he said. "They want a holiday that is curated around their interests and supported throughout the journey." TravClan's cofounder and chief executive Arun Bagaria gave the other half of the logic: India is becoming one of the largest outbound travel markets in the world, and people taking their first international trips account for a significant part of that growth. Outbound travel means Indians travelling abroad, as opposed to inbound tourists visiting India.

TravClan will use the money for global travel supply, technology and destination expansion. It also plans to hire in business development, engineering and destination teams. Bagaria said the partnership lets his company reach more Indian consumers, and that contracting directly with suppliers and running its own ground teams is how it keeps prices competitive while maintaining service levels.

The financial terms are the largest unknown. No source in these articles gives a valuation, a stake percentage or a purchase price. It is also not clear whether Flipkart's investment gives it any board seat, exclusivity or right of first refusal over TravClan's supply. And there is no figure for how much of Flipkart's travel volume, if any, currently flows through TravClan.

The strategic context is Flipkart's pattern of building businesses outside its core marketplace. It has been pushing into food delivery and event ticketing, and it is spending heavily on quick commerce through Flipkart Minutes. Ahead of this year's Big Billion Days sale, the company said it had expanded its dark store network to nearly 1,200 Flipkart Minutes micro-fulfilment centres across more than 150 cities. Dark stores are small warehouses closed to walk-in customers, used only to fulfil online orders. Flipkart expects to add another 1,000 of them by mid-2027 or by the Big Billion Days sale of 2027. Travel sits alongside those bets rather than replacing them.

The risk in this particular deal is that minority stakes buy influence, not control. Flipkart is not acquiring TravClan, so it cannot direct the startup's roadmap, and rival travel platforms could in principle strike similar arrangements with the same supplier network. Travel is also a low-margin, service-heavy business where problems surface on the ground, in visa delays or a hotel that does not honour a booking, not in the app. Flipkart is relying on TravClan's destination teams to absorb that. The eternal complement

06 Oct 2026

The essay argues that human progress has always depended less on raw genius than on the machinery and bureaucracy needed to turn ideas into reality, and that AI is about to change which of those two things is scarce. The authors point to the James Webb Space Telescope: Galileo widened our view of the universe with two lenses and a tube, while Webb required eighteen mirror segments engineered to fifty nanometer precision, three hundred organizations across fourteen countries, and ten billion dollars. That gap is the point. Research productivity has been falling even as spending rises. Sustaining Moore's law now takes more than eighteen times as many researchers as it did in the early 1970s, and economy-wide research productivity fell by a factor of forty-one while research effort rose twenty-three-fold. The technician workforce is growing twice as fast as the scientists.

The authors call the unglamorous capacity to execute "institutional intelligence," and treat it as a complement to frontier intelligence: more of one raises the value of the other. Their bet is that AI will spend most of its time on the execution side, writing code, searching literature, turning sketches into prototypes. That would let one person pursue projects that once needed a whole organization, and push creative work outside the institutions that currently gatekeep it. The thing to watch is whether that produces a wave of independent work or just a new set of bottlenecks. My view: the essay is right that execution, not ideas, is the binding constraint, and that is exactly why the incumbents with the biggest bureaucracies stand to gain most.
