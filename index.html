Bitcoin order books deepen a year after $19 billion crash

10 Oct 2026

Bitcoin's order book is deeper now than on any date measured since the start of 2025. About $11.7 million sits within 1% of the price, roughly 75% more than on crash day a year earlier and up from about $9 million at the start of this year. Depth is the value of resting buy and sell orders near the current price, so a deeper book absorbs larger trades with less price movement. Bitcoin trades about one-third cheaper than before the crash, so the improvement reflects more capital from market makers, not just lower coin values. Ether's recovery is stronger in tight bands, with depth within 0.5% of price more than doubling to about $4.2 million.

Altcoins went the other way. Dollar depth in a basket of altcoins is down about a third since the start of 2025 at 5% from price, to around $2 million. Spot trading has not recovered either. Weekly spot volume on centralized exchanges averaged about $279 billion over the four weeks to Sept. 27, nearly two-thirds below the $801 billion traded in the week of the crash. Activity bottomed in August at around $135 billion and has doubled since, but remains well short of crash levels.

The rebuilt books got an early test this week. As the market sold off, bitcoin's 1% depth fell about 12% between Oct. 7 and Oct. 8. For holders, the split matters: majors have deeper liquidity to absorb selling, while altcoins and spot activity still lag.

Leverage and crowded bets still pose crash risk a year later

10 Oct 2026

The conditions that caused the October 2025 crash have not gone away. Bitcoin plunged from around $122,000 to $105,000 in minutes on Oct. 10, 2025, triggering roughly $19 billion in liquidations. Coming into that selloff, open interest was near historic highs and traders had piled into bullish positions, betting bitcoin would follow its four-year cycle toward fresh records. Mark Connors of Risk Dimensions said positioning was important then and remains important today. He argued the move was driven by derivatives, not onchain data, showing paper bitcoin still governs near-term prices.

Perpetual futures, which let traders bet on price moves without owning bitcoin, remain a major part of crypto trading. Exchanges have strong financial incentives to keep offering leveraged products. Connors warned another October 10th is still possible because levered products have not gone away. He also said the four-year cycle tied to halving mining rewards has changed and can no longer be relied on for as much signal.

Chris Sullivan of Hyperion Decimus advised avoiding leverage and watching open interest, funding rates and market sentiment. Open interest tracks outstanding derivatives contracts, while funding rates reflect the cost of holding perpetual futures positions. For long-term holders, he recommended buying, moving coins off exchanges and holding them in self-custody. Connors said the market bent but did not break, and that traders are now more attentive to market structure.
